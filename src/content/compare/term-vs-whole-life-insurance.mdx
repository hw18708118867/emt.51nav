## The core tradeoff

**Term life insurance** covers you for a set period—often 10, 20, or 30 years—and pays a death benefit only if you die during that window. **Whole life insurance** covers you for your entire life and builds a cash value you can borrow against or withdraw. Term is pure protection; whole life is protection plus a forced savings component.

The practical difference shows up in price. For the same death benefit, term is dramatically cheaper because it only covers the risk of death during the term and includes no savings. Whole life costs far more per month because part of every premium funds the cash value and the lifetime coverage.

## When term usually wins

For most people with dependents, term is the better fit:

- **The need is temporary.** You usually need the most coverage while kids are young, the mortgage is large, and income is still climbing. After that, you may be able to self-insure.
- **The price is manageable.** The money you save versus whole life can be invested yourself, often producing more wealth than the whole-life cash value.
- **It is simpler.** You buy coverage for a period, pay for it, and move on.

If your goal is to replace income so your family stays stable if you die early, term does that job directly. The [Life Insurance Calculator](/calculators/life-insurance-calculator/) estimates how much you might need and for how long.

## When whole life can make sense

Whole life is not useless; it serves narrower purposes:

- **Permanent need.** If someone will always depend on you—a child with special needs, a lifelong dependent—lifetime coverage matters.
- **Estate liquidity.** Some use it to provide tax-advantaged cash for heirs to cover estate costs.
- **Forced saving.** If you will not invest the difference yourself, the cash value acts as a disciplined account, though usually at a low effective return.
- **Guaranteed rates.** The growth and death benefit are contractually fixed, which appeals to risk-averse buyers.

The catch is cost and opacity: fees are baked in, the early cash value grows slowly, and surrendering early can lose money.

## A quick numbers example

Suppose a healthy 35-year-old wants $500,000 of coverage. A 20-year term policy might cost roughly $30 a month. A whole life policy for the same amount could cost $400 or more a month.

Over 20 years, term costs about $7,200 total. The whole life policy costs roughly $96,000 over the same span—money that could instead be invested. Even investing the $370 monthly difference at a modest return can leave you with more than the whole-life cash value, while the term policy covered the exact years your family was most exposed.

This is why "buy term and invest the difference" is a common recommendation—though it only works if you actually invest the difference.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Buying whole life when term fits.** Paying for lifetime coverage and cash value you do not need strains the budget and leaves less for other goals.
- **Letting a policy lapse early.** Whole life surrenders can return far less than paid in during the first years.
- **Confusing cash value with savings.** The cash value grows after fees and is not the same as putting money in a brokerage account you control.
- **Skipping the amount math.** Whether term or whole, size the benefit to real obligations—see [life insurance basics](/guides/life-insurance-basics) for the DIME method.

## The real question

The choice is not "which is better insurance" but "what problem am I solving?" If it is income replacement for a finite period, term is usually the efficient answer. If it is permanent coverage or a disciplined cash vehicle for a specific estate need, whole life has a place.

Most households are better served by adequate term plus their own invested savings than by an expensive whole life policy they may struggle to keep.
