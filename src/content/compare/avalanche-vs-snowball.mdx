## Avalanche: better math

The debt avalanche method sends every extra dollar to the debt with the highest interest rate first, while you keep making minimum payments on everything else. Because interest is usually the part that drags balances upward, attacking the costliest debt first almost always produces the lowest total interest and the shortest payoff path on paper.

A quick example: a $5,000 credit card at 24% and a $3,000 personal loan at 9%. Avalanche throws the extra cash at the card. Across a typical payoff, that choice can save hundreds of dollars in interest compared with spreading the same extra payment evenly. The bigger the gap between your highest and lowest rates, the larger avalanche's advantage.

## Snowball: better momentum

The debt snowball method ignores rates and targets the smallest balance first. You clear it, feel the win, and roll that payment into the next debt. The math is slightly worse, but the psychology is better — and for many people, simply sticking with the plan matters more than saving a little interest.

## Which one should you choose

Pick avalanche if your main goal is minimizing cost and you are comfortable watching a large balance sit untouched for a while. Pick snowball if motivation has been the reason past debt plans fell apart.

If you hold a mix of high-rate and tiny balances, a common middle path works well: knock out one or two tiny debts first for momentum, then switch to avalanche for the rest. When your rates are close together, the two methods converge anyway, so pick the one you will actually follow.

## A worked example with the numbers

Take the same $5,000 at 24% and $3,000 at 9%, with $300 a month available beyond the minimums. Avalanche attacks the card: the high-rate balance falls fast, and because less interest accrues each month, more of every payment goes to principal. Snowball clears the $3,000 loan first for the quick win, but the card keeps compounding at 24% the whole time. Over the full payoff, avalanche typically finishes earlier and saves real money — often a few hundred dollars — while snowball buys momentum you can see in month one.

## When the rate gap is small

If your debts sit at 18% and 20%, the avalanche advantage shrinks to almost nothing. In that case, pick the method you will stick with, because the interest saved by optimizing is smaller than the risk of quitting. Method adherence beats marginal math.

## The hybrid that most people should consider

Knock out one or two tiny balances first — say, a $400 medical bill — to feel progress, then switch to avalanche for the large, high-rate debts. This captures most of the snowball's motivation with most of the avalanche's efficiency. It is not "cheating" either method; it is using both where each works best.

## Track the plan so it sticks

Write down the order, the target payoff date, and the monthly amount. Cross debts off as they close. A visible list turns an abstract intention into a scoreboard, which is exactly the psychological fuel the snowball method relies on.

## What if you have many debts

With five or more balances, simplify first: list every debt with its rate and minimum. Decide your method once, then automate the extra payment so the decision is made for you each month. The biggest failure mode with many debts is analysis paralysis, not method choice.

## The wrong choice is no system

Both strategies crush the default approach: making random extra payments with no fixed order and no monthly target. Random payments feel busy but rarely shorten the timeline much.

Whatever you choose, keep every minimum payment current. Missing one erases more progress than any method decision, and it can trigger fees and rate hikes that undo weeks of work.

## Why the method beats the tool

No app or spreadsheet makes the plan work; the order you commit to does. Once you pick avalanche or snowball, the gains come from sending the extra payment every month without exception, not from optimizing the math. People who switch methods every few months lose the momentum both approaches rely on. The discipline of a fixed, automated extra payment matters more than which label you chose, and it is the part most budgeting tools cannot enforce for you.

## A note on very high rates

When one debt carries a very high rate — say 25% or more — avalanche's edge becomes large enough that the psychological comfort of snowball rarely justifies the extra cost. In that case, attacking the highest rate first is both the math and the behavior win. Reserve snowball for situations where rates are close and motivation is the binding constraint.

## What not to do

A few habits quietly undo either method. Paying only the minimums on every card keeps the balances alive for years. Spreading the extra payment evenly across all debts, with no fixed order, removes the focus that makes avalanche and snowball work. And ignoring the highest-rate balance while celebrating small wins can leave the most expensive debt growing in the background. The choice between the two methods matters far less than simply having a method and sticking to it.
