## The core tradeoff

With a **Roth IRA**, you pay income tax on your contributions now and then take qualified withdrawals completely tax-free later — no tax on the growth, ever. With a **Traditional IRA**, your contribution may be tax-deductible this year, which lowers your current bill, but every dollar you withdraw in retirement is taxed as ordinary income.

Both accounts share the same annual contribution limits, so the choice is not about how much you can save. It is purely about *when* you pay the tax. Roth bets that your rate today is lower than it will be later; Traditional bets the opposite.

## When Roth tends to look stronger

Roth usually wins when:

- you are early in your career and your income (and tax bracket) is still climbing
- you expect other taxable income in retirement — a pension, Social Security, a 401(k) — and want at least one pool you can pull from tax-free
- you value flexibility: Roth contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn anytime without penalty, which quietly makes the account a backup emergency buffer

One note: direct Roth contributions phase out at higher incomes, so very high earners may be shut out or need a backdoor path.

## When Traditional can be compelling

Traditional contributions shine when the deduction moves the needle this year — for example, it drops you into a lower bracket, or it keeps you under an income threshold that protects other deductions or credits. If you are already in a high bracket and expect to spend down to a lower one in retirement, deferring the tax is often the better math.

A catch worth remembering: Traditional IRAs are subject to required minimum distributions once you reach your early seventies, forcing taxable withdrawals whether you need the cash or not. Roth IRAs have no RMD during your lifetime, which also makes them cleaner to pass to heirs.

## A third option people miss

You do not have to pick one forever. Many people contribute to both, or do a **Roth conversion** in a low-income year to shift future growth into the tax-free bucket. The right mix shifts as your income changes.

## A quick example with real numbers

Suppose you contribute $6,500 a year and it grows into $50,000 by retirement. In a Roth, that $50,000 comes out completely tax-free. In a Traditional, the same $50,000 is taxed as ordinary income when you withdraw it. If you are in the 22% bracket in retirement, the Traditional leaves you about $39,000 of real spending power from that pool, while the Roth leaves the full $50,000. The Roth's edge is the tax you never paid on the growth.

The flip side is just as real. If you are in the 24% bracket today and drop to 12% in retirement, the Traditional deduction saved you 24% up front and you only pay 12% later — a clear win for Traditional. The account choice is really a guess about two tax rates separated by decades.

## State tax adds another layer

Federal rules get the attention, but state income tax can quietly change the math. Some states do not tax Roth withdrawals at all, which strengthens Roth for residents there. Others tax Traditional withdrawals but gave you no deduction when you contributed, which weakens Traditional. If you might retire in a different state than the one you live in now, that uncertainty is one more reason not to bet everything on a single account.

## What if you change your mind later

You are not locked in. A Roth conversion lets you move Traditional money into Roth in any year you choose, paying tax on the converted amount at your current rate. People often do this in a low-income year — between jobs, during a sabbatical, or after a market dip — when the tax cost is temporarily small. Conversions are permanent, so they reward patience and timing more than urgency.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Assuming Roth is always better because "tax-free" sounds nice, without checking your current bracket.
- Forgetting that Traditional required minimum distributions start in your early seventies, which can push you into higher taxable income later.
- Contributing to a Roth directly when your income is too high to do so, then discovering the contribution was invalid.
- Ignoring that you can split contributions across both accounts in the same year, up to the shared limit.

## The real question

The comparison is not "which account is better." It is a bet on your future tax rate and a preference for certainty now versus flexibility later. If you cannot predict your retirement income with confidence, splitting between the two is a reasonable hedge.

## Watch the income limit on direct Roth

Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher incomes, and the exact thresholds shift with inflation each year. If your income is near the line, contributing directly can be disallowed or partially limited, which is why some higher earners use the backdoor Roth path instead. The income limit applies to the Roth side specifically; it does not change the shared annual ceiling, it only changes who may use the Roth portion of it.
