## Why this check matters

The Social Security Administration says it calculates your monthly retirement and disability benefit by looking at how much you have earned. That is why your earnings record matters more than most people expect.

If the record is wrong, the problem is not only paperwork. It can affect the benefit calculation built on top of that record.

## What the earnings record actually is

Your record is the year-by-year history of taxable earnings the SSA has on file for your Social Security number. The benefit formula uses your highest 35 years of earnings, indexed for wage growth. A single missing or understated year does not ruin the calculation, but several gaps can quietly lower the monthly amount you eventually receive — and you will not notice until you are ready to claim.

## How the record goes wrong

Errors are more common than they should be:

- an employer reports your wages late or to the wrong number
- a typo in your Social Security number sends earnings to someone else's file
- a name change after marriage was not matched to your old earnings
- a year of self-employment income was never reported

None of these are dramatic, and that is exactly why they slip through. The SSA generally catches obvious mismatches, but it is not infallible, and the correction is far easier while the paper trail is fresh.

## Why errors ripple into your benefit

Because the formula weights your top 35 years, a missing high-earning year can pull your average down. Lower average earnings mean a lower primary insurance amount, which is the base for every monthly benefit you will ever collect. The cost of one ignored error can repeat across decades of retirement checks.

## What to do if you find a mistake

Start with the online account, where you can flag a discrepancy. If the issue traces to an employer, you may need pay stubs, W-2s, or tax returns from the affected year to prove what was earned. The further back the error, the more documentation helps — which is why the annual August check is smarter than a panic review right before you claim.

## How often to check

You do not need to turn it into a monthly ritual. An annual review around the time SSA recommends is often enough for most workers. People within ten years of claiming may want to check every year or two, since the record becomes more decisive as the claim date approaches.

## The timing the SSA points to

SSA says you should **check your record in August** to make sure last year's amount is correct.

That detail is useful because it gives you a simple recurring habit:

- wait until the prior year's wages should be reflected
- review the new amount
- correct mistakes while the records are easier to trace

## Where to check it

SSA says you can sign in to your account, check your record, or create an account if you do not have one yet.

If you do not want to use the online route, SSA also says you can mail a request for your Social Security Statement instead.

That gives most people two paths:

1. use the online account for a quicker review
2. request the statement by mail if needed

## What to look for

The review itself is simple:

- make sure last year's amount appears
- confirm the amount looks right
- investigate missing or obviously wrong earnings

You do not need to turn it into a monthly ritual. An annual review around the time SSA recommends is often enough for most workers.

## Why this connects to retirement planning now

This is not only for people on the edge of retirement. A clean earnings record helps you plan more accurately years before benefits begin.

If the earnings record is wrong, later planning based on Social Security estimates can also be off.

## What the benefit formula actually rewards

The formula is not a straight average of everything you ever earned. It uses your highest 35 years, adjusted for wage growth so a dollar earned decades ago is measured against today's wage levels. Two consequences follow. First, years with very low or zero earnings — time out of the workforce, a sabbatical, early part-time years — pull the average down, which is why keeping the record accurate in those years matters. Second, the longer you work, the more high-earning years replace low ones, so the record you fix today keeps paying off decades later at claim time.

## Time the check around life changes

Certain events make an early review worth it. A name change after marriage, a gap in work, or switching between employed and self-employed can all affect how earnings are reported and matched. If any of those happened in the last year or two, do not wait for the annual August check; confirm the record now while the documents that prove the earnings are easiest to find.
