## Start with the right website

The CFPB says you can request a free copy of your credit report from each of the three major consumer reporting companies by visiting **AnnualCreditReport.com**. The CFPB also notes that you may be able to view updated credit reports online more frequently at no cost.

That matters because many people still land on lookalike sites, paid monitoring funnels, or "free" offers that are not the standard legal path.

## Why this matters before a major purchase

The CFPB says you should check your credit report before a major purchase that may involve a loan, such as a house or a car. It also says you should review your report to spot possible identity theft and before applying for a new job in situations where a credit report may be reviewed.

In other words, checking a report is not only about credit score curiosity. It is a screening step before decisions that can affect borrowing cost, approval odds, or background checks.

## What to review once you pull the report

The CFPB highlights several common error types to look for:

- identity information that is wrong
- accounts that do not belong to you
- closed accounts shown as open
- late payments reported incorrectly
- balances or credit limits that look wrong
- the same debt listed more than once

This is the useful part of the process. Pulling the report is easy. Reviewing it carefully is where the value comes from.

## How to space your checks

The CFPB notes that you can request all three reports at once or spread them out. Spacing them across the year can make monitoring easier because it gives you more than one checkpoint instead of a single annual review.

That is a simple habit with a clear benefit:

- all three at once if you are preparing for a mortgage or auto loan
- staggered if you are mostly monitoring for changes or errors

## Report versus score

A credit report is the underlying data — your accounts, balances, and payment history. A credit score is a number a company calculates from that data. You can fix problems using the report even if you never see the score. Chasing a score without reading the report is like dieting by watching the scale and ignoring what you eat.

## How errors actually cost you

An account wrongly marked late, or a balance shown too high, can push you into a worse rate tier on a mortgage or auto loan. Over a multi-year loan, even a small rate increase can cost hundreds or thousands of dollars. That is the concrete reason to treat report review as a money task, not a curiosity.

## Signs of identity theft to watch

Beyond simple errors, watch for:

- accounts you never opened
- inquiries from lenders you did not contact
- an address or name you do not use
- a sudden change in utilization you did not cause

Any of these can mean someone is using your identity, and the report is often the first place it shows up.

## Why the free path is enough

Paid monitoring can be useful, but it is not required to stay on top of your reports. The free annual reports plus the more frequent no-cost access many bureaus now offer cover the basics. Paying monthly for a service you could replicate for free is a recurring cost that rarely improves the outcome.

## What to do if something is wrong

If you find an error, the CFPB says you should contact both the credit reporting company that issued the report and the company that provided the information. If the problem continues, you can also submit a complaint to the CFPB.

Treat suspicious information as a task, not just a frustration. Document it and start the dispute process while the details are fresh.

## Make the review a habit, not a panic

The value of a free report fades if you only pull it once after something goes wrong. Building a light routine — one bureau per four months, or all three before any major borrowing — turns the report into an early-warning system instead of a post-mortem. Errors caught a year early are far easier to dispute than those found days before a mortgage application, when there is no time to fix them. The free access exists precisely so you can check often without cost, which is the whole point of using it.

## Freezes are separate from monitoring

A credit freeze, which is free, restricts access to your file so new accounts cannot be opened in your name, and it is a strong step if you suspect identity theft. It is different from monitoring, which only alerts you after something happens. Freezing does not block your existing accounts or your own access to credit; it simply stops new applications. Knowing the difference helps you pick the right tool when something on the report looks wrong.
