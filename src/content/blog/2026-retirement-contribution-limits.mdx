## The headline numbers for 2026

The IRS says the **2026 IRA contribution limit is $7,500**, and the limit is **$8,600 if you are age 50 or older**. The IRS also says the **basic elective deferral limit for 401(k)-style plans is $24,500** in 2026.

Those are the numbers most savers care about first because they tell you how much room you have before you run into annual contribution caps.

## What people often miss

The IRA limit is a **combined limit across all of your traditional and Roth IRAs**, not a separate cap for each account. The IRS also notes that if your taxable compensation is lower than the annual limit, your contribution limit is reduced to that lower amount.

That means a saver with multiple IRA accounts should think in terms of one shared annual bucket, not several independent ones.

## Why the limit is useful even if you cannot max it out

The annual limit is not only for high earners trying to max every account. It also gives ordinary savers a planning ceiling.

If you know the upper bound, you can work backward:

- monthly target needed to stay on pace
- whether a bonus or tax refund should go into retirement
- whether a raise should partly increase retirement savings

Even moving closer to the limit can matter. You do not have to hit the ceiling for the ceiling to be useful.

## The 401(k) catch-up for older savers

Workers age 50 or older can make an additional catch-up deferral on top of the $24,500 base 401(k) limit, which materially raises the ceiling for late-career savers. The same age-based catch-up idea applies to IRAs, where the $8,600 figure already bakes in the extra amount. If you are in the catch-up window, the gap between "what I can save" and "what I assumed" widens considerably, and the limit becomes a more powerful planning tool.

## How the 401(k) and IRA limits interact

The two limits are separate, not shared. You can contribute up to the IRA cap across your IRAs and, in the same year, up to the 401(k) cap through your workplace plan. That means a maxed-out saver can use both ceilings in one year. The IRA combined limit and the 401(k) limit do not offset each other; they stack.

## The order of operations

For most people the efficient sequence is:

1. contribute at least enough to a 401(k) to capture the full employer match — that is free money and usually the highest return available
2. consider an IRA if you want broader investment choices or a Roth option your plan lacks
3. go back to the 401(k) to reach the higher base limit if you still have room and cash flow

Chasing the IRA limit before the employer match is usually a mistake, because the match is a guaranteed return the limit cannot replicate.

## What counts as eligible compensation

Not every dollar of income can be contributed. The IRS ties these limits to taxable compensation — wages, salaries, tips, and similar earned income. Investment income, pensions, and most passive income do not create contribution room. That is why a high earner with mostly investment income may still face a low or zero contribution limit, while a wage earner with the same total income has full room.

## What to check before increasing contributions

The IRS also reminds taxpayers that traditional IRA deductions can be limited if you or your spouse are covered by a retirement plan at work and your income is above certain levels. Roth IRA contributions can also be limited by income.

In most cases, the order should be:

1. confirm how much room you have under the 2026 limits
2. check whether workplace-plan participation affects IRA deductions
3. check whether income affects Roth eligibility
4. automate the increase if the account type still fits

## One move if you want to save more in 2026

If maxing an IRA or 401(k) sounds unrealistic, do not make the mistake of turning the whole exercise into an all-or-nothing decision.

Pick one small increase you can actually sustain:

- raise a paycheck deferral percentage
- set a monthly IRA transfer
- route part of a bonus or refund into retirement

For most households, consistency matters more than a one-time heroic contribution.

## Small increases compound across decades

The gap between contributing a little and contributing close to the limit is not just about one good year. An extra $2,000 a year from age 30 to 65, growing tax-deferred, becomes a substantially larger balance than the same dollars spent elsewhere, because the growth has decades to build. The annual limit is not a target everyone must hit, but it is a ceiling that makes the power of starting early concrete. Even modest, consistent contributions near the limit do far more than sporadic max-effort years.
