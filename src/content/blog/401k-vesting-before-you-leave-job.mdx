## Why this catches people off guard

People often know how much they have contributed to a 401(k), but they are much less clear on how much of the employer money is actually theirs yet.

That gap matters most when someone is about to leave a job.

Your own salary deferrals are not usually the issue. The bigger question is whether the employer match or other employer contributions are already vested, or whether part of that balance could be left behind if you leave too soon.

## What vesting actually means

The IRS says vesting means ownership.

In plain terms, it answers a simple question: if you left the company now, how much of the employer-funded portion of the account would you be entitled to keep?

The IRS also says your own employee contributions are always 100% vested. That means the money you contributed from your paycheck is yours. The possible gray area is usually the employer side of the account.

## Why the type of 401(k) matters

This is where people often assume all plans work the same way when they do not.

The IRS says that in safe harbor 401(k) plans and SIMPLE 401(k) plans, required employer contributions are always fully vested. But in a traditional 401(k), employer contributions can vest over time according to the plan's vesting schedule.

So before you make any move, it is worth finding out which kind of plan you are actually in. A person with a safe harbor plan and a person with a traditional plan may be looking at the same account screen and still have very different answers.

## The part to check before you resign

The U.S. Department of Labor says the Summary Plan Description is one of the key documents to review if you want to understand when benefits become vested.

That is the document that usually gives you the cleanest answer on:

- whether the plan uses cliff vesting or graded vesting
- how years of service are counted
- whether all employer contributions follow the same schedule

If you are close to a vesting breakpoint, that detail can matter more than people expect.

## Why timing can matter more than the headline balance

Two workers can both have a large employer match balance showing on their statements and still walk away with different amounts.

That is because the statement balance and the vested balance are not always the same thing.

The practical question is not just, "How much is in the account?"

It is also:

"How much of the employer-funded portion is mine if I leave this month?"

Sometimes the answer is all of it. Sometimes the answer is only part of it. And occasionally the difference is large enough that it is worth checking before you finalize a departure date.

## A simple pre-exit checklist

Before leaving a job, check these items:

1. whether your plan is traditional, safe harbor, or SIMPLE
2. your current vested percentage on employer contributions
3. the plan's vesting schedule in the Summary Plan Description
4. whether another completed service period would change the vested amount soon

This is not a reason to stay in a bad job longer than you should. It is just the kind of detail that is better understood before the decision becomes irreversible.

## What to do after you know the vested amount

Once you know the vested amount, the next planning step gets easier.

You can think more clearly about whether to leave the money in the old plan, roll it into an IRA, or move it into a new employer plan if that option makes sense.

But that choice comes after the first question. First make sure you know what portion of the balance is really yours to move.
