## Key takeaway

Compound interest is interest that earns interest: your gains start earning their own gains. Start early, even with small amounts, and time does most of the heavy lifting.

## Compound interest is interest on your interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount you put in. If you deposit $1,000 at 5% simple interest, you earn $50 every year, forever—the interest never builds on itself.

Compound interest is different. You earn interest on your principal, and then the next period you earn interest on the principal plus all the interest already added. The old interest becomes part of the new base. That is the whole idea in one sentence: **your money makes money, and then that money makes money too.**

A simple way to see it: $1,000 at 5% compounded annually earns $50 in year one. In year two you earn 5% on $1,050, not $1,000—so $52.50. The extra $2.50 is interest on interest. Small at first, but it grows because the base keeps getting bigger.

## How compounding shows up in real accounts

Compounding is everywhere once money earns a return:

- **Savings and high-yield accounts:** interest posts monthly or daily and immediately starts earning the next period.
- **Certificates of deposit (CDs):** a fixed rate compounds on a schedule until maturity.
- **Retirement and brokerage accounts:** dividends and price growth reinvest, so your balance compounds even without new deposits.
- **Debt works against you the same way:** a credit card balance compounds when interest is added to a balance you have not paid, so the amount you owe can grow on its own.

The frequency matters but usually not as much as people expect. The same 5% rate compounded daily is only a hair better than compounded monthly, because the rate is already low. What matters far more is the rate itself and how many years it runs.

## A worked example you can copy

Suppose you invest $200 a month at a 7% annual return, compounded monthly, for 30 years. You personally put in $72,000 ($200 × 360 months). But the balance at the end is roughly $244,000. The difference—about $172,000—is almost entirely compounding: gains earning gains, month after month, for three decades.

Now compare starting ten years later. Same $200 a month, same 7%, but only 20 years. You contribute $48,000 and end near $104,000. You put in $24,000 less, but you gave up about $140,000 of eventual balance. That gap is what compounding does when you give it more time.

You can run the same shape of calculation for any goal. Take the monthly amount you can spare, pick a reasonable long-term return, and let the timeline do the work. ## The most common mistake: underestimating time

The biggest error is thinking compound interest is about finding a higher rate. A higher rate helps, but time is the steeper lever. Going from 7% to 9% over 30 years is a meaningful boost, but starting 10 years earlier at the same 7% usually beats both.

Other mistakes:

- **Stopping contributions during downturns:** pausing resets the base and wastes the cheapest shares.
- **Ignoring fees:** a 1% annual fee does not sound like much, but over 30 years it can quietly take a large slice of the compounded total.
- **Confusing APY with the rate you "feel":** a savings APY already includes compounding, so comparing APYs is the honest comparison—see [what APY means](/guides/what-is-apy) for the details.
- **Forgetting debt compounds too:** the same math that builds wealth also builds balances you carry.

## How to put compounding to work

You do not need to be an investing expert to benefit. A few steps:

1. **Start with the first dollar you can spare.** The date you begin matters more than the amount at the start.
2. **Automate the contribution** so the habit survives busy months.
3. **Reinvest everything**—dividends, interest, bonuses—rather than spending the gains.
4. **Keep costs low** so more of the return compounds instead of leaking to fees.
5. **Use a long horizon** for money you will not touch, and a safe account for money you might need soon.
