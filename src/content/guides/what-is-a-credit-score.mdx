## Key takeaway

A credit score is a three-digit summary of how you have handled borrowed money, used by lenders to decide whether to approve you and at what rate. Payment history and amounts owed make up most of it.

## What a credit score actually measures

A credit score is a number—most commonly from 300 to 850—that predicts how likely you are to repay borrowed money on time. It is not a judgment of your worth; it is a risk estimate built from your credit history. Higher is better: a higher score usually means easier approval and lower interest rates.

In the U.S., the FICO and VantageScore models are the most common. They use similar inputs and produce similar ranges, so the habits that help one help the other. You do not need to obsess over the exact brand; you need to understand what feeds the number.

## The factors behind the score

For the FICO model, the main ingredients are:

- **Payment history (about 35%):** Have you paid past bills on time? This is the single biggest factor.
- **Amounts owed (about 30%):** How much of your available credit are you using? Lower utilization is better.
- **Length of credit history (about 15%):** Older, established accounts help.
- **Credit mix (about 10%):** Having different types—card, installment loan—can help a little.
- **New credit (about 10%):** Several applications in a short window can ding the score temporarily.

Notice that income, savings, and how much you have in the bank are not in the formula. A score measures borrowed-money behavior, not wealth.

## How utilization works in practice

Credit utilization is your balances divided by your credit limits. If you have a $10,000 limit and a $3,000 balance, your utilization is 30%. Generally, keeping utilization low—many aim for under 30%, and lower is better—supports a higher score.

A useful quirk: utilization has no memory. Paying a balance down this month can lift the score next month, because issuers usually report the balance on the statement date. This is why paying before the statement closes, or spreading charges across multiple cards, can help the reported number even if the spending is the same.

## Why the score matters

Lenders use it for more than credit cards:

- **Mortgages and auto loans:** a better score can mean a meaningfully lower rate, which changes the total cost by thousands.
- **Apartment rentals and some jobs:** landlords and a few employers check credit as a stability signal.
- **Insurance and deposits:** in some states, insurers and utilities use credit-based scores to set rates or deposit requirements.

## Common mistakes

- **Closing an old card you no longer use.** That can shorten your history and raise utilization, both of which can lower the score.
- **Applying for several cards at once.** Each application can add a hard inquiry, and a cluster looks risky.
- **Only checking the score, not the report.** Errors on the report—wrong accounts, old balances—are what drag scores down, and you fix those at the report level.
- **Assuming no debt means a great score.** Thin or empty credit history can leave you with a low or no score, because there is little to judge.

## How to build and protect your score

1. **Pay every bill on time.** Set autopay for at least the minimum so a forgotten due date never costs you.
2. **Keep balances low** relative to limits, and pay before the statement date when you can.
3. **Keep old accounts open** unless there is a real reason to close them.
4. **Check your reports annually** for errors and dispute anything wrong.
5. **Apply for new credit only when you need it.**
