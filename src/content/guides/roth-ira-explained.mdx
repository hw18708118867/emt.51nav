## What makes a Roth IRA different

A Roth IRA is a retirement account with a single defining feature: you contribute money you have already paid tax on, and in return, qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free.

That includes the growth. Every dollar of investment gains the account earns over the decades can come out without owing income tax, as long as you follow the rules. For a young saver with a long runway, that untaxed growth can be the most valuable part of the whole arrangement.

## Roth versus traditional: when do you pay the tax?

The choice between a Roth and a traditional retirement account comes down to timing.

- A **traditional** account gives you a tax deduction now and taxes your withdrawals later.
- A **Roth** account gives you no deduction now but makes your withdrawals tax-free later.

Which account wins depends on whether your tax rate is likely to be higher now or in retirement.

A Roth tends to win when you expect to be in a similar or higher tax bracket later, which is common for people early in their careers whose income will rise. It also wins for anyone who simply values certainty: tax-free income in retirement is predictable, while future tax rates and rules are not.

A traditional account can be the better deal when you are in a high bracket today and expect a lower one in retirement, because the upfront deduction is worth more to you now.

## The contribution rules that actually matter

Roth IRAs come with a few rules that are worth knowing before you open one:

- **Annual contribution limit.** There is a yearly cap on how much you can add, set by the IRS and adjusted over time, with a higher limit if you are age 50 or older. This limit is shared across all your IRAs combined, traditional and Roth, not per account.
- **Income limits.** The ability to contribute to a Roth phases out at higher incomes. Above certain thresholds you may be limited or unable to contribute directly.
- **Earned income requirement.** You generally need earned income, like wages, to contribute, and you cannot contribute more than you earned that year.

Because these numbers change, check the current year's official figures before planning your contributions. A calculator will happily project any amount you enter, but the IRS sets the real ceiling.

## The five-year rule and withdrawals

Roth withdrawals have their own timing rules, and the most important is the **five-year rule**.

To take earnings out completely tax-free, two things generally need to be true: the account has been open at least five years, and you are at least 59½. Meet both and qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free.

There is helpful flexibility on the contribution side, though. Because you already paid tax on your contributions, you can usually withdraw the amount you put in at any time without taxes or penalties. It is the earnings that carry the stricter rules. This makes a Roth more flexible than many people assume, though raiding it early still costs you the future tax-free growth.

## Why a Roth pairs well with a 401(k)

A Roth IRA and a workplace 401(k) are not an either-or choice; they complement each other.

A common, effective order of operations looks like this:

1. Contribute to your 401(k) at least up to the full employer match, because that match is free money.
2. Then fund a Roth IRA, for its tax-free growth and broader investment choices.
3. If you still have room to save, go back and contribute more to the 401(k).

This sequence captures the guaranteed return of the match first, then layers in the long-term tax advantages of the Roth. It also gives you tax diversification: some money taxed now, some taxed later, which leaves you more flexibility in retirement.

## What tax-free growth is really worth

The value of a Roth is easiest to see over long horizons. A few thousand dollars contributed each year, left to compound for several decades, can grow into a balance many times larger than the contributions themselves. In a traditional account, a chunk of that growth would be taxed on the way out. In a Roth, none of it is.

That is the trade you are making: you give up a deduction today in exchange for shielding decades of compounding from tax. The longer the money has to grow, the better that trade tends to look, which is why a Roth is so often recommended for younger savers.
