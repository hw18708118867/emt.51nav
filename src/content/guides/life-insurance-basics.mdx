## Life insurance replaces income when someone dies

The core purpose of life insurance is to replace the economic value of a person's life if they die prematurely. It's not an investment, not a savings vehicle first and foremost—it's a risk management tool.

When you buy life insurance, you agree to pay regular premiums, and the insurance company agrees to pay a death benefit to your beneficiaries if you die while the policy is in force. That money can help your family pay the mortgage, cover living expenses, fund college, replace lost income, or settle final expenses.

## Term vs permanent life insurance

Most life insurance policies fall into two broad categories:

- **Term life insurance:** Covers you for a specific period (the "term"), usually 10, 15, 20, or 30 years. If you die during the term, the benefit is paid. If you outlive the term, the coverage ends. Term is usually the simplest and most affordable option because it only covers the risk of death with no savings component.
- **Permanent life insurance:** Covers you for your entire life, as long as you pay the premiums. It includes a cash value component that grows over time, which you can borrow against or withdraw from. Permanent life insurance can be whole life, universal life, or variable life, each with different features and costs.

For most people, term life insurance is enough because the need for life insurance is usually temporary—until your kids are grown, until your mortgage is paid off, until you have enough savings to self-insure.

## How much coverage you actually need

The right amount of life insurance depends on what you want it to cover. A common starting point is to think about:

- **Income replacement:** Multiply your annual income by 10-12 times, or by the number of years until you expect to retire.
- **Debt payoff:** Add your mortgage balance, student loans, and other debts you want to eliminate.
- **Future expenses:** College tuition for your kids, funeral costs, or other specific goals.
- **Existing savings:** Subtract any savings or investments that could be used immediately.

The "DIME" method (Debt, Income, Mortgage, Education) is a simple framework for estimating the right coverage amount. The goal isn't to leave your family rich—it's to leave them financially stable.

## What affects your life insurance premium

Life insurance premiums are based primarily on mortality risk—the likelihood that the insurer will have to pay a death benefit while the policy is in force. The main factors include:

- **Age:** Younger people usually pay less because they're statistically less likely to die soon.
- **Health:** Your medical history, current health conditions, and lifestyle factors like smoking or heavy drinking affect rates.
- **Gender:** Women typically pay less than men because they have longer life expectancies on average.
- **Coverage amount:** More coverage means higher premiums.
- **Policy type:** Term is usually cheaper than permanent coverage.
- **Occupation and hobbies:** Risky jobs or hobbies (like skydiving or rock climbing) can increase rates.

This is one reason to buy life insurance earlier rather than later: locking in a lower rate while you're younger and healthier can save money over the life of the policy.

## The application and underwriting process

Getting life insurance usually involves:

1. **Choosing a policy type and coverage amount:** Deciding what you need and how long you need it.
2. **Completing an application:** Answering questions about your health, family history, lifestyle, and finances.
3. **Taking a medical exam (for many policies):** A nurse or paramedic comes to your home or office to take your vitals, draw blood, and collect a urine sample.
4. **Waiting for underwriting:** The insurance company reviews your application and medical information to set your rate.
5. **Accepting the policy:** Once approved, you sign the policy documents and set up your premium payments.

Some policies offer "no-exam" or simplified underwriting, which can be faster but usually more expensive. For most people, the lower cost of fully underwritten coverage is worth the extra time.

## Naming and updating beneficiaries

Your beneficiaries are the people who will receive the death benefit if you die. It's important to:

- Name primary beneficiaries (who get the benefit first) and contingent beneficiaries (who get it if the primary can't).
- Be specific—using full names rather than just "my spouse" or "my kids" can avoid confusion.
- Update beneficiaries after major life events like marriage, divorce, the birth of a child, or the death of a beneficiary.
- Keep beneficiaries separate from your will, because life insurance proceeds usually pass outside of probate.

Beneficiary designations usually override what's in your will, so it's worth reviewing them every few years to make sure they still reflect your wishes.

## What life insurance doesn't cover

Life insurance is a powerful tool, but it has limits. Most policies don't pay out if:

- You die by suicide within the first two years of the policy.
- You lie on your application and the insurer discovers the misrepresentation within the contestability period (usually two years).
- You die while committing a felony.
- The policy lapsed because you stopped paying premiums.

Outside of these exceptions, most deaths are covered, including from accidents, natural causes, and most illnesses.

## Life insurance is part of a broader safety net

Life insurance works best when it's part of a complete financial safety net that includes an emergency fund, disability insurance (which replaces income if you can't work due to illness or injury), and a will or estate plan.

The goal isn't to buy the most expensive policy or the maximum coverage—it's to buy enough coverage, for the right length of time, at a price you can afford.
