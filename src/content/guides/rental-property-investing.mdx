## Rental property can provide income and appreciation, but it's not passive

Rental property investing involves buying a property with the intention of renting it out to tenants rather than living in it yourself. Done well, it can generate monthly cash flow, build equity through mortgage paydown, and potentially appreciate in value over time. Done poorly, it can become a financial drain that ties up your money and your time.

The key difference between a good rental property and a bad one is usually the math—whether the income from rent covers all the expenses and leaves enough left over to be worth the risk and effort.

## Cash flow is the first thing to calculate

Cash flow is the money left over each month after collecting rent and paying all the expenses. The basic formula is:

**Cash flow = Rent collected - (Mortgage payment + Taxes + Insurance + Maintenance + Property management + Vacancy + Utilities)**

Positive cash flow means the property puts money in your pocket each month. Negative cash flow means you have to put money in to keep it going. Many investors aim for positive cash flow from day one, because counting on appreciation alone is risky.

## The 1% rule as a quick filter

A common starting point for evaluating a rental property is the 1% rule: the monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price.

For example:
- A $300,000 property should rent for at least $3,000 per month
- A $200,000 property should rent for at least $2,000 per month

This is just a quick filter, not a guarantee of a good investment. It helps you narrow down properties that are worth doing deeper analysis on. Properties that don't meet the 1% rule might still work in some markets, but they require extra scrutiny.

## Cap rate measures return independent of financing

The capitalization rate, or cap rate, is the rate of return on a property if you paid cash for it. The formula is:

**Cap rate = Net Operating Income (NOI) / Purchase price**

NOI is the annual income from rent minus operating expenses (not including the mortgage payment). For example, if a property generates $30,000 per year in net operating income and costs $400,000 to buy, the cap rate is 7.5%.

Cap rates let you compare properties across different markets without worrying about how each is financed. A higher cap rate generally means a higher return, but sometimes also higher risk.

## Cash-on-cash return considers your actual investment

Cash-on-cash return measures the annual return on the actual cash you put into the property (your down payment plus closing costs and initial repairs). The formula is:

**Cash-on-cash return = Annual before-tax cash flow / Total cash invested**

For example, if you put $80,000 down on a property and it generates $4,800 per year in cash flow, your cash-on-cash return is 6%.

This is a useful measure because it reflects the actual return on your invested money, considering that you're using leverage (a mortgage) to control a larger asset.

## The five main expenses people underestimate

New investors often forget to budget for all the expenses of owning a rental property:

1. **Vacancy:** Even in good markets, units turn over. Budget 5-10% of rent for periods when the property is empty.
2. **Maintenance and repairs:** Things break—roofs, appliances, HVAC systems. Budget 10-15% of rent annually for maintenance.
3. **Property management:** If you don't want to manage the property yourself, expect to pay 8-12% of rent for a property manager.
4. **Capital expenditures:** Big-ticket items like a new roof or HVAC system that come up every 10-20 years. Set aside money monthly for these.
5. **Insurance and taxes:** Landlord insurance is usually more expensive than homeowner insurance, and property taxes can go up.

Underestimating these expenses is one of the most common reasons rental properties become negative cash flow.

## Appreciation is possible but not guaranteed

Many rental property investors hope for appreciation—the property increasing in value over time. Appreciation can come from:

- **Market appreciation:** The overall real estate market going up, which you can't control.
- **Forced appreciation:** Making improvements to the property that increase its value, which you can control.

Appreciation is a nice bonus when it happens, but it's risky to count on it as the main way to make money from a rental property. The best investments usually work even if appreciation is modest or nonexistent.

## Equity buildup happens automatically (with a mortgage)

When you have a mortgage on a rental property, your tenants are effectively paying it down for you through their rent payments. Each month, part of the mortgage payment goes to principal (building your equity) and part goes to interest.

This equity buildup is a form of passive wealth building, but it only works if the rent covers the mortgage payment and other expenses.

## Landlord responsibilities are real work

Owning a rental property is rarely completely passive. Even with a property manager, you'll still need to:

- Make big decisions about repairs and improvements
- Review financial statements
- Handle evictions if necessary (though the property manager usually does this)
- Keep up with landlord-tenant laws in your area

If you manage the property yourself, you'll also need to find tenants, collect rent, handle maintenance calls, and deal with any issues that come up. It's not a full-time job, but it's work.

## Location matters more than almost anything else

The best rental properties are usually in areas with:

- Strong job growth and population growth
- Low vacancy rates
- Good schools (if renting to families)
- Amenities like shopping, restaurants, and transportation
- Reasonable property taxes and landlord-friendly laws

A nice property in a bad location is rarely a good investment. A mediocre property in a great location often is.

## Financing a rental property is different from a primary home

Mortgages for rental properties usually have stricter requirements:

- Higher down payment (often 20-25% minimum)
- Higher interest rates
- More stringent credit score requirements
- You might need to show that you have reserves covering several months of mortgage payments

Lenders see rental properties as riskier than primary homes, so they charge more and require more protection.

## Rental property fits into a broader investment portfolio

Rental property can be a good addition to an investment portfolio, but it shouldn't be your only investment. It's illiquid (hard to sell quickly), requires active management, and ties up a lot of capital in one asset.

The best rental property investors usually have a diversified portfolio that includes stocks, bonds, and other investments alongside real estate.
