## A savings account that trades access for a fixed rate

A certificate of deposit is a deposit you agree to leave untouched for a set period, in exchange for a fixed interest rate that is usually higher than a regular savings account. You hand the bank $10,000 for, say, two years; it pays you a guaranteed rate the whole time and hands back your money plus interest when the term ends.

The trade is simple to state. You give up easy access to the cash for the length of the term. In return you get a rate that will not drop even if the bank lowers rates elsewhere, and your principal is protected, federally insured at banks within standard limits.

## Rate, APY, and why they differ

A CD quotes an interest rate, but the number that matters for comparison is the APY, the annual percentage yield. APY folds in compounding, the interest you earn on your interest, so it is slightly higher than the stated rate and reflects what you actually take home over a year.

A $10,000 CD at 4.5% compounded monthly grows to roughly $10,940 after two years. The headline rate is 4.5%, but the effective yield is a touch higher once compounding is counted. When you compare CDs across banks, line up the APYs rather than the stated rates, since different compounding schedules can make two same-rate CDs pay differently.

## The cost of breaking the term

The fixed rate comes with a string attached: an early-withdrawal penalty. Take the money out before the term ends and the bank typically charges several months of interest, sometimes more on longer CDs. That penalty can wipe out much of what you earned, and on a short-held CD it can even eat into principal.

This is the feature to respect. A CD is not a place for money you might need on short notice. It is for cash you can confidently leave alone until a known date.

## Where a CD fits, and where it does not

CDs suit money with a deadline and a low tolerance for risk. A down payment you will use in eighteen months, a tax bill due next year, cash you want kept safe and predictable rather than invested. The fixed rate gives you certainty about exactly what you will have when the term ends.

Two places a CD usually is not the answer:

- **An emergency fund.** Emergencies do not schedule themselves around your CD's maturity date. That money belongs somewhere you can reach instantly, like a high-yield savings account, even if the rate is a little lower.
- **Long-term growth.** Over many years, CD rates have historically trailed what a diversified investment portfolio returns. For retirement-horizon money, the safety of a CD comes at the cost of growth you likely want.

A common middle path is a CD ladder: split the money across CDs with staggered maturities so a portion frees up regularly while the rest keeps earning the higher long-term rate.
