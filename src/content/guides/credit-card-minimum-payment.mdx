## Key takeaway

Paying only the minimum on a credit card can stretch a balance out for decades and multiply what you repay. If you can, pay more than the minimum—even a little more—to cut years and interest off the bill.

## What the minimum payment actually is

The minimum payment is the smallest amount your card issuer will accept that month to keep your account in good standing. It is usually calculated as a percentage of your balance—often around 2% to 3%—or a flat floor like $25 or $35, whichever is greater.

That number is designed to keep you paying, not to help you finish. Because it is a small slice of the balance, most of it goes to interest and only a little chips away at what you actually owe. The issuer is happy for you to keep paying for a long time.

## Why the minimum is so expensive

Credit cards typically charge high APRs, often in the low 20s. When you pay only the minimum, interest keeps piling onto the remaining balance every month, and the next minimum is based on that larger balance. You can get stuck in a slow loop where the balance barely moves.

A simple illustration: a $5,000 balance at 22% APR with a 2% minimum could take well over 20 years to pay off and cost roughly double the original balance in interest. The exact numbers depend on your rate and the issuer's formula, but the shape is always the same—minimum payments are built for slowness.

## A worked example you can check

Say you owe $3,000 at 24% APR and your minimum is 2% of the balance (with a $25 floor). Early on your minimum is about $60 a month. Of that, roughly $60 in interest accrues in the first month, so almost nothing reduces the principal. The balance lingers near $3,000 for a very long time.

Now change one thing: pay $100 a month instead of the minimum. The extra $40 above the minimum starts cutting the principal directly. The payoff date moves from decades away to a few years, and the total interest drops by a large margin. ## Common mistakes with the minimum

- **Treating the minimum as a budget.** It is the floor, not a plan. Paying it feels responsible but rarely makes progress.
- **Adding new charges while paying minimums.** New spending replaces whatever little principal you paid, so the balance never falls.
- **Ignoring the APR.** A 0% promo card is different; a standard high-APR card makes minimum payments especially costly. If several cards are involved, the [Debt Payoff Calculator](/calculators/debt-payoff-calculator/) helps you sequence them.
- **Assuming the minimum stays constant.** As the balance falls, the percentage-based minimum falls too, which slows things further unless you hold your payment steady.

## How to break out of the minimum loop

You do not need extra income to escape—you need to pay a fixed amount above the minimum and keep it there:

1. **Pay the same dollar amount every month**, even as the minimum shrinks. If you paid $120 this month, pay $120 next month, not the new lower minimum.
2. **Put any windfall at the balance**—a tax refund, bonus, or gift—rather than spreading it thin.
3. **Stop adding new charges** to the card until it is paid, then pay it off in full each month.
4. **Consider a lower-rate option** carefully: a balance transfer or a personal loan can cut the interest, but only helps if you actually pay it down instead of re-spending.
5. **Use the calculator first.** See the real payoff date at your minimum versus a higher fixed payment, then pick the payment you can sustain.
