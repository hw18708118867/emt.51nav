## Key takeaway

An auto loan is a secured installment loan where the car is the collateral. The payment depends on price, down payment, rate, and term—and a longer term usually costs more overall even when the monthly payment looks smaller.

## What an auto loan is

An auto loan is money you borrow to buy a vehicle, repaid in fixed monthly installments over a set term, usually two to seven years. The car itself secures the loan, which means the lender can repossess it if you stop paying. Because the loan is secured, rates are typically lower than on unsecured credit cards but higher than on a mortgage.

The loan has three moving parts: the amount you borrow, the interest rate (APR), and the term (how long you take to repay). Change any one and the monthly payment and total cost both shift.

## How the payment is built

Your monthly payment covers two things: a slice of the principal (the amount borrowed) and the interest for that month. Early in the loan, most of the payment is interest; later, more goes to principal. This is the same amortization pattern as a mortgage, just on a shorter clock.

A down payment reduces the amount you borrow, which lowers both the payment and the total interest. Trading in an old car counts toward this. The loan amount is the price minus down payment, plus any fees or taxes rolled in, minus trade-in value.

The [Auto Loan Calculator](/calculators/auto-loan-calculator/) shows how rate and term change the payment. Two examples:

- A $30,000 car with $5,000 down, at 7% for 60 months, borrows $25,000 and costs roughly $495 a month.
- The same loan at 72 months drops the payment to about $425, but you pay interest for an extra year and usually at a slightly higher rate.

## The term trap

Longer terms are tempting because they lower the monthly number. But they almost always raise the total cost and create a period where you owe more than the car is worth (negative equity). A car loses value fast in the first years, while a long loan pays down principal slowly—so you can be underwater on the loan even as the car ages.

A common guardrail is the 20/4/10 rule: at least 20% down, a loan no longer than 4 years, and total car costs under 10% of your income. You can test whether a payment fits using the [Car Affordability Calculator](/calculators/car-affordability-calculator/).

## Common mistakes

- **Focusing only on the monthly payment.** A dealer can lower the payment by stretching the term, which costs you more. Negotiate the price and rate, not just the payment.
- **Rolling fees and extras into the loan.** Add-ons financed into the loan earn interest too, and you may pay for them long after the car is gone.
- **Ignoring your credit profile.** Your rate depends heavily on it; a stronger file can mean a much lower APR. See [what drives your car payment](/guides/what-drives-your-car-payment) for the full breakdown.
- **Skipping the total cost.** Always ask the out-the-door price and the total of payments, not just the monthly figure.

## How to approach your next car loan

1. **Check your budget first** with an affordability calculator so a dealer's offer does not set your ceiling.
2. **Get preapproved** at a bank or credit union before visiting a dealer, so you know the rate you should beat.
3. **Put down as much as you can** without draining your emergency fund, to shrink the loan and the interest.
4. **Choose the shortest term you can afford**, to save on total interest and avoid being underwater.
5. **Compare the APR, not just the payment**, across lenders before signing.
