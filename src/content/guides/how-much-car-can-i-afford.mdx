## Car affordability is about monthly cash flow, not just sticker price

A car is usually the second-largest purchase most people make after a home, but it's a depreciating asset—its value goes down over time, not up. That means affordability is less about the total price and more about what fits comfortably in your monthly budget without crowding out other financial goals.

The mistake many people make is focusing only on the monthly payment. A longer loan term can make the payment feel affordable even as the total cost of the car quietly climbs higher with interest.

## The 20/4/10 rule as a starting point

A common guideline for car affordability is the 20/4/10 rule:
- Put at least **20% down**
- Finance for no longer than **4 years (48 months)**
- Keep total monthly car costs (payment + insurance + gas + maintenance) under **10% of your gross income**

This rule isn't perfect for everyone, but it's a useful check. It prevents you from stretching the loan too long, makes sure you have some equity from day one, and keeps car costs from taking over your budget.

For example, if your gross income is $6,000 per month, you'd aim to keep total car costs under $600 per month, with a down payment of at least 20% of the car's price and a loan term no longer than 48 months.

## Why the down payment matters

Putting money down on a car does two important things:

1. **It reduces your monthly payment:** Every dollar you put down is a dollar you don't have to borrow and pay interest on.
2. **It helps you avoid being "underwater" or "upside down":** Because cars depreciate quickly, putting 20% down makes it less likely that you'll owe more on the loan than the car is worth in the first few years.

Being underwater matters because if you need to sell the car or get into an accident that totals it, you could end up owing money on a car you no longer have.

## The true cost of car ownership is more than the payment

The monthly loan payment is only part of what a car costs. You also need to budget for:

- **Insurance:** Coverage requirements vary by state, but liability, collision, and comprehensive can add significantly to the monthly cost, especially for newer or more expensive cars.
- **Gas:** Depends on how much you drive and the car's fuel efficiency.
- **Maintenance and repairs:** Oil changes, tires, brakes, and unexpected repairs add up. A good rule of thumb is to set aside $50-$100 per month for these costs.
- **Registration and fees:** Annual registration, inspections, and sometimes tolls.
- **Parking:** If you live in a city or pay for parking at work, this can be a meaningful monthly expense.

When estimating affordability, add these up along with the loan payment to get the full picture of what the car actually costs you each month.

## How loan term affects total cost

Longer loan terms lower the monthly payment, but they usually increase the total amount you pay for the car because you pay interest for more years.

For example, financing $30,000 at 6% interest:
- 48 months: ~$705/month, total interest ~$3,840
- 60 months: ~$580/month, total interest ~$4,800
- 72 months: ~$500/month, total interest ~$6,000

That 72-month loan saves about $200 per month compared to the 48-month loan, but it costs an extra $2,160 in total interest. You're also stuck with the payment for two extra years, which could delay other financial goals.

## New vs used vs certified pre-owned

Each option has tradeoffs for affordability:

- **New cars:** Come with a warranty, the latest features, and no unknown history—but they depreciate the most the moment you drive off the lot.
- **Used cars:** Usually better value because someone else took the initial depreciation hit, but you have to research the car's history and might face higher maintenance costs sooner.
- **Certified pre-owned (CPO):** Typically inspected, come with a limited warranty, and are often only a few years old—but they cost more than non-certified used cars.

For many people, a slightly used car (2-3 years old) hits the sweet spot between reliability, features, and affordability.

## How your credit score affects your rate

Your credit score is one of the biggest factors in the interest rate you get on a car loan. A higher score usually means a lower rate, which saves money every month and over the life of the loan.

Before applying for a car loan, it's worth checking your credit report for errors and knowing roughly what rate you might qualify for. A difference of even one or two percentage points can add up to thousands of dollars over the life of the loan.

## Trading in vs selling privately

If you have a car to replace, you have two main options:

- **Trading in:** Convenient and reduces the amount you need to finance, but you usually get less for your car than you would selling it privately.
- **Selling privately:** Usually gets you more money, but it takes more time and effort.

Whether the extra money is worth the effort depends on how much your car is worth, how comfortable you are with the process, and how much time you have.

## Car affordability is about tradeoffs

The most expensive car you can get approved for is rarely the car you can actually afford without straining your budget. A good affordability check is to ask: does this car leave enough room in my budget for saving, investing, and enjoying life outside of transportation?

A car is a tool, not a status symbol. The best car for you is usually the one that meets your needs, fits your budget, and lets you still make progress toward other financial goals.
