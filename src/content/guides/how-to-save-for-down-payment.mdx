## Key takeaway

A down payment is the cash you bring to a home purchase, and a bigger one lowers your loan, your monthly payment, and often your interest rate. The fastest way to save it is a dedicated account, a fixed monthly transfer, and a clear target.

## A down payment is your first slice of ownership

When you buy a home, the lender covers most of the price and you cover the rest up front. That up-front cash is the down payment. A $400,000 home with a 20% down payment means you bring $80,000 and borrow $320,000.

Why it matters:

- **Smaller loan:** every dollar down is a dollar you do not pay interest on for 30 years.
- **Lower monthly payment:** less borrowed means less principal and interest each month.
- **Avoiding PMI:** put down less than 20% on a conventional loan and you usually pay private mortgage insurance until you reach 20% equity—an extra cost with no equity to show for it.
- **Better rate:** larger down payments look lower-risk to lenders and can earn a slightly better rate.

You do not always need 20%. Many loans allow 3% to 5% down, and government loans can go lower. But the trade-off is real: a smaller down payment means higher payments and likely PMI.

## How much you actually need

The target is more than the percentage. Add:

- **The down payment itself** (e.g., 20% of price).
- **Closing costs**, typically 2% to 5% of the loan.
- **Moving and setup** cash so you are not house-poor on day one.

A worked example: on a $350,000 home, a 20% down payment is $70,000. Add about $9,000 in closing costs and $5,000 for moving and initial repairs, and your real target is near **$84,000**, not $70,000. People who forget closing costs come up short.

## A savings plan you can actually follow

Suppose you need $84,000 and have three years. That is $28,000 a year, or about **$2,333 a month**. If that feels impossible, you have two levers: extend the timeline or lower the target price.

The math is simple and the [Down Payment Calculator](/calculators/down-payment-calculator/) shows how the monthly amount shifts with price, rate, and down-payment percentage. The habit is the hard part, not the arithmetic.

## The mistake everyone makes: mixing the fund with daily cash

The down-payment fund should sit apart from your checking account. Money you can see gets spent. Put it in a high-yield savings account so it earns a little and stays mentally "locked."

Other errors:

- **Ignoring PMI in the comparison:** a 5% down payment looks cheaper now but costs more monthly for years.
- **Borrowing the down payment:** a loan for the down payment adds a payment you must still qualify around.
- **Forgetting the timeline:** starting 12 months late can mean a smaller home or a higher rate.
- **Draining the emergency fund:** your house will need repairs; keep a cash buffer separate.

## How to build it

1. **Pick a target price and down-payment percentage**, then add closing costs—use the [Down Payment Calculator](/calculators/down-payment-calculator/) to set the number.
2. **Open a separate high-yield account** just for this goal.
3. **Automate the transfer** on payday so saving happens before spending.
4. **Redirect windfalls**—tax refunds, bonuses, gifts—straight into the fund.
5. **Re-check the price** as you near the goal; the market may have moved.
