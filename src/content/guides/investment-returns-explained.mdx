## Why "what return did I get" is a harder question than it sounds

Say you put in $10,000 and a few years later it is worth $18,000. Did you do well?

You cannot answer that yet, because the number on its own is missing the one thing that decides everything: time. An $8,000 gain in two years is excellent. The same gain spread over fifteen years is barely keeping up with inflation.

That is why investors lean on a small set of return measures instead of one. Each answers a slightly different question, and knowing which one you are looking at keeps you from comparing things that should not be compared.

## Total ROI: the simplest view

Return on investment, or ROI, is the most basic measure. It is just the percentage gain from start to finish:

(final value − amount invested) ÷ amount invested

Turning $10,000 into $18,000 is an $8,000 gain on $10,000, or 80% ROI.

ROI is useful because it is intuitive and it makes the size of the gain obvious. The catch is that it says nothing about how long the money was at work. Two investments can both show 80% ROI while one took two years and the other took twenty. As a single number for a single holding period, ROI is fine. As a way to compare opportunities, it is incomplete.

## Annualized return: putting everything on a per-year basis

To compare fairly, you need the return expressed as a steady yearly rate. That is the annualized return, and it answers: "what constant yearly rate would have produced this result?"

For the example above, 80% total over five years works out to roughly 12.5% per year, not 16%. It is lower than simply dividing 80% by five because returns compound. Each year's growth builds on a slightly larger base, so you need a smaller annual rate to reach the same finish line.

Annualized return is the number that lets you line up a quick trade and a long hold side by side. A 30% gain in one year (30% annualized) actually beat a 90% gain spread over ten years (about 6.6% annualized), even though 90% looks bigger at a glance.

## CAGR: annualized return for a start and end value

You will often see the term CAGR, which stands for compound annual growth rate. For most practical purposes, CAGR and annualized return describe the same idea: the smooth yearly rate that connects a beginning value to an ending value.

CAGR shines when you are measuring something that grew from one figure to another over a known number of years, like a portfolio balance, a fund's value, or even revenue. The formula is:

(ending value ÷ beginning value) raised to the power of (1 ÷ years), minus 1

The reason CAGR is more honest than a simple average of yearly returns is compounding. Imagine an investment that drops 50% one year and gains 50% the next. The simple average is 0%, which sounds like a wash. But $100 falls to $50, then rises to $75, a real loss of 25%. CAGR captures that; a naive average hides it.

## Where each measure fits

- Use **ROI** when you want the headline gain on a single investment over a single period, and time is not the main question.
- Use **annualized return or CAGR** when you are comparing investments with different time spans, or judging performance against a benchmark like a broad market average.
- Use a **compound growth projection** when you are adding money over time, because none of the three measures above handles ongoing contributions well.

That last point is important. ROI, CAGR, and annualized return all assume a single lump sum that you leave alone. The moment you are contributing every month, the math changes, and a compound interest projection is the better tool.

## What these numbers leave out

Every return measure on this page is a clean abstraction, and real investing is messier:

- **Taxes** can take a meaningful bite out of gains in a regular brokerage account, though not in tax-advantaged accounts.
- **Fees** lower returns every year, and small percentages compound against you just as growth compounds for you.
- **Timing of cash flows** matters. If you added or withdrew money partway through, simple ROI and CAGR will misstate your actual experience.
- **Volatility** is invisible in a CAGR. Two portfolios with the same CAGR can feel completely different to live through if one swung wildly along the way.

None of this makes the measures useless. It just means they describe the shape of a return, not the full lived reality of it.

## A simple habit that keeps you honest

Whenever you see a return figure, ask two questions: over what period, and is it total or annualized? Those two questions catch most of the misleading comparisons people make, including the ones in marketing material that quote a big total return without mentioning it took a decade to earn.

Once you are in the habit of converting everything to an annual rate before comparing, returns stop being a source of confusion and become a useful way to judge where your money is best put to work.
