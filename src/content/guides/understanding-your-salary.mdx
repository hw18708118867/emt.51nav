## Key takeaway

Your salary is the headline number, but what lands in your bank account is smaller. Knowing the gap between gross and net—and how raises, overtime, and benefits change it—turns a job offer into a real budget.

## Gross vs net is the first thing to learn

Your salary is usually quoted as an annual gross figure: what you earn before anything is taken out. Your net pay, or take-home, is what hits your account after taxes, insurance, and other deductions.

A simple split:

- **Gross:** the full number on the offer letter.
- **Pre-tax deductions:** 401(k), health insurance, HSA—these lower your taxable income.
- **Taxes:** federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and possibly state and local tax.
- **Net:** what is left, your real spending money.

Someone offered $70,000 may take home closer to $52,000 after a 401(k) contribution, health premiums, and taxes. The [Salary Calculator](/calculators/salary-calculator/) converts the annual number into a monthly and biweekly take-home so the budget is built on reality, not the headline.

## How pay frequency changes the feel

The same annual salary paid weekly, biweekly, or monthly is the same money, but the rhythm differs. Biweekly means 26 paychecks a year—two months have three, which is a useful saving opportunity people miss. Monthly is steadier but leaves less buffer between checks.

A worked example: $60,000 a year is $5,000 a month, but biweekly it is about $2,308 per check × 26 = $60,008—the extra two checks are real money if you do not spend them by accident.

## The mistake everyone makes: judging an offer by gross

People compare two offers by the big number and ignore the net. A higher gross with worse benefits or higher state tax can leave less take-home than a lower gross offer.

Other errors:

- **Forgetting the 401(k) match is part of pay:** a match is free money and lowers taxable income at once.
- **Ignoring overtime and bonus variability:** a base-plus-variable job is not the base alone.
- **Treating a raise as all take-home:** a $5,000 raise may add only ~$3,500 after tax and deductions.
- **Overlooking benefits as income:** health, HSA, and PTO have dollar value beyond salary.

## How to use your salary well

1. **Convert to net first** with the [Salary Calculator](/calculators/salary-calculator/) before building any budget.
2. **Capture the extra paychecks** if you are paid biweekly—direct them to savings or debt.
3. **Max the match** in your 401(k); it raises total comp and cuts tax.
4. **Model a raise** before lifestyle creep sets in—decide where the new net goes first.
5. **Count benefits as income** so you compare job offers on the full package.
