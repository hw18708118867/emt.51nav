## What refinancing actually does

Refinancing replaces your current mortgage with a new one, usually to get a lower interest rate. You take out a fresh loan, use it to pay off the old balance, and from then on you make payments on the new loan instead.

The appeal is obvious: a lower rate means a lower monthly payment and less interest over time. But refinancing is not free, and a lower rate alone does not guarantee it is worth doing. The real decision comes down to a single question: do the savings outweigh the cost of getting them?

## Weigh the savings against the closing costs

Refinancing comes with closing costs, often a few thousand dollars, covering things like the new loan's origination, appraisal, and title work. You are spending that money upfront to lock in lower payments going forward.

The break-even point tells you when you come out ahead. The math is straightforward:

closing costs ÷ monthly savings = months to break even

If refinancing costs $6,000 and saves you $250 a month, you break even in 24 months. Stay in the home past that point and every month is pure savings. Sell or refinance again before then, and you lost money on the deal.

So the question is not only whether the new rate is lower, but whether you will keep the loan long enough to earn the closing costs back. If you plan to move in a year, even a great rate may not pay off. If you are settled for the long haul, a smaller rate drop can still be very much worth it.

## The trap of resetting the clock

One detail is easy to miss: refinancing usually restarts the loan term.

Say you are seven years into a 30-year mortgage, with 23 years left. If you refinance into a fresh 30-year loan, you have just stretched your remaining balance back out over 30 years again. The monthly payment drops, partly from the lower rate and partly from spreading the balance over more years. But total interest can actually rise, because you are paying interest for longer.

This does not make refinancing a bad idea. It just means you should compare **lifetime interest**, not only the monthly payment. Two ways to avoid the trap:

- Refinance into a **shorter term** (say, a 15- or 20-year loan) so you are not extending the payoff date.
- Keep the lower payment but **pay extra** toward principal, effectively recreating your old payoff timeline at the new lower rate.

## When refinancing usually makes sense

A refinance tends to be worth a serious look when several of these are true:

- The new rate is meaningfully lower than your current one, not just a fraction.
- You plan to stay in the home well past the break-even point.
- Your credit and finances are strong enough to qualify for the best rates.
- You can refinance without erasing the progress you have already made on the term.

There are also reasons to refinance beyond rate alone, such as switching from an adjustable-rate loan to a fixed one for stability, or removing mortgage insurance once you have enough equity. Pulling cash out of equity is another use, though it increases what you owe and deserves its own careful look.

## When to hold off

Refinancing is probably not worth it when:

- You might move or sell before reaching break-even.
- The rate improvement is small after closing costs are factored in.
- Restarting the term would add more total interest than the rate saves.
- Your current loan is nearly paid off, so there is little balance left for a lower rate to help.

A lower advertised rate is the bait, not the decision. Run your specific numbers, including the closing costs and how long you will realistically keep the loan, before committing.
