## Key takeaway

An IRA lets you save for retirement with tax advantages, but how much you can contribute each year is capped and can phase out as your income rises. Knowing the limit—and whether a traditional or Roth IRA fits—turns a vague "save more" into a concrete annual number.

## An IRA is a tax-advantaged wrapper around your savings

An Individual Retirement Arrangement (IRA) is an account, not an investment. You put cash in, choose what it invests in, and the account type decides the tax treatment.

- **Traditional IRA:** contributions may be tax-deductible now; growth is untaxed until withdrawal, when it is taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth IRA:** you contribute with after-tax dollars, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

Both have the same annual contribution cap, set by the IRS each year. For most working adults the limit is a fixed dollar amount per person, with an extra catch-up amount allowed once you reach age 50.

## The contribution limit is not the whole story

The cap is the max you can put in, but two rules shrink it in practice:

- **Earned income cap:** you can only contribute up to your taxable compensation for the year. No job income, no IRA contribution (with a spouse workaround for non-working partners).
- **Income phase-out on Roth:** above a certain modified adjusted gross income, the amount you can put into a Roth IRA is reduced and eventually eliminated. High earners use a "backdoor" Roth, which is more advanced and worth a separate look.

A traditional IRA deduction can also phase out if you or your spouse has a workplace plan and your income is above set thresholds. So the real question is not just "what is the limit?" but "how much of it applies to me?"

## A worked example

Suppose the annual IRA limit is $7,000 and you are 45 with $90,000 of earned income. You can contribute up to $7,000. If you are single with a workplace plan and your income is above the traditional deduction phase-out range, your contribution is still allowed—but the deduction may be limited, which pushes you toward Roth if you qualify.

If you are 52, the catch-up adds another $1,000, so your cap becomes $8,000. The [IRA Contribution Calculator](/calculators/ira-contribution-calculator/) applies the phase-outs to your exact numbers so you do not guess.

## The mistake everyone makes: confusing the account with the investment

People open an IRA and stop at the cash. An empty IRA earns nothing. The account is only as good as what you buy inside it.

Other errors:

- **Missing the deadline:** you can usually contribute for a tax year until the filing deadline (around mid-April), so last year's slot may still be open.
- **Over-contributing:** exceeding the cap triggers a penalty on the excess every year it sits there.
- **Ignoring the Roth income limit:** a contribution that later proves ineligible creates a tax mess.
- **Forgetting the backdoor:** high earners who assume "Roth is closed to me" may miss a legal path.

## How to use an IRA well

1. **Find your real limit** with the [IRA Contribution Calculator](/calculators/ira-contribution-calculator/) before you fund.
2. **Pick traditional vs Roth** based on your current vs expected future tax rate.
3. **Fund before the deadline**—use the prior-year slot if it is still open.
4. **Invest the cash** in something diversified; a parked balance helps no one.
5. **Automate** a monthly transfer so you hit the cap without a year-end scramble.
