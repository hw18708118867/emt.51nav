## The debt avalanche targets the highest interest first

The debt avalanche is a debt payoff method where you pay off your debts from highest interest rate to lowest, regardless of balance. You make minimum payments on all debts, then put any extra money toward the debt with the highest interest rate first. When that's paid off, you roll the money you were putting toward it into the next highest-rate debt, and so on.

The "avalanche" refers to how your payment power grows as you eliminate high-interest debt first, saving you the maximum amount in interest over time.

## How the debt avalanche works step by step

To use the debt avalanche method:

1. **List all your debts** (except your mortgage, usually) from highest interest rate to lowest, ignoring the balances.
2. **Make the minimum payment** on every debt every month.
3. **Put any extra money you can find** toward the highest-interest debt on the list.
4. **When the highest-interest debt is paid off**, take the money you were putting toward it and add it to the minimum payment for the next highest-rate debt.
5. **Repeat the process** until all debts are paid off.

The key is that you're targeting the debt that's costing you the most money each month, which minimizes the total interest you pay over the life of all your debts.

## Why the order matters for interest savings

The debt avalanche is built on math first. High-interest debt—like credit cards with 18-25% APR—grows faster than almost anything else in your financial life. Every extra dollar you put toward it saves you more in interest than the same dollar put toward a lower-rate debt.

This means the avalanche usually gets you out of debt for less total money than any other method, as long as you can stick with the plan.

## An example of the debt avalanche in action

Suppose you have these debts:
- Credit Card A: $800 balance, 22% APR, $25 minimum payment
- Credit Card B: $2,500 balance, 18% APR, $60 minimum payment
- Personal Loan: $7,000 balance, 10% APR, $150 minimum payment
- Car Loan: $12,000 balance, 6% APR, $280 minimum payment

Total minimum payments: $515 per month. If you can put an extra $200 toward debt each month ($715 total), you'd:

1. Pay off Credit Card A first (highest rate, 22%), even though it has a small balance
2. Then put that $25 + $200 extra toward Credit Card B (next highest rate, 18%)
3. Then put that $60 + $225 extra toward the personal loan (10%)
4. Finally, put that $150 + $285 extra toward the car loan (6%)

In this example, the order looks the same as the snowball because the highest-rate debt also happens to be the smallest. But if the highest-rate debt was the largest balance, the avalanche would still target it first.

## When the debt avalanche shines

The debt avalanche is particularly effective if:

- You have one or more debts with very high interest rates (15%+)
- You're motivated primarily by saving money rather than seeing quick wins
- You have a history of sticking with financial plans even when progress feels slow
- The difference in interest rates between your debts is significant

For people who can stay disciplined, the avalanche is usually the cheapest way to become debt-free.

## What the debt avalanche requires in discipline

The tradeoff with the avalanche is that progress can feel slower at first, especially if your highest-interest debt also has a large balance. You might go months or even years before paying off your first debt, which can make it harder to stay motivated.

This is why the avalanche works best for people who can stay focused on the long-term goal of saving money rather than needing immediate psychological rewards.

## How to handle a large high-interest debt

If your highest-interest debt also has a large balance, you can still make progress feel more tangible by:

- Tracking your balance reduction month by month
- Celebrating milestones (like paying off 25%, then 50%, then 75% of the balance)
- Visualizing how much interest you're saving each month
- Remembering that every extra payment is making a real difference in total cost

Even if the full payoff feels distant, each month brings you closer, and the savings are real.

## Why a small emergency fund still matters

Just like with the snowball, it's usually wise to keep a small starter emergency fund ($500-$1,000) while using the avalanche. The last thing you want is to make great progress on high-interest debt only to have to put a surprise expense right back on a credit card.

The emergency fund is a safety net that protects your progress, even if it means slowing down the debt payoff slightly at first.

## The debt avalanche is about math, but behavior still matters

The avalanche is the most mathematically efficient method, but efficiency only matters if you actually stick with the plan. If you start with the avalanche but find yourself losing motivation because progress feels too slow, it's okay to switch to the snowball or a hybrid approach.

The goal isn't to prove you can handle the most difficult plan—it's to actually become debt-free.

## Choosing between avalanche and snowball

You don't have to pick strictly one or the other. Some people use a hybrid:

- Pay off one small debt first for a quick win, then switch to the avalanche
- Split extra money between the highest-rate debt and a small debt
- Focus on the avalanche but build in small celebrations along the way

The best choice is the one that keeps you moving forward without abandoning the plan entirely.
