## Key takeaway

Debt consolidation rolls several balances into one loan or card, usually to simplify payments or lower the rate. It helps when the new rate is genuinely lower and you stop adding new debt—otherwise it just reshuffles the problem.

## What debt consolidation means

Debt consolidation is taking several existing debts—often credit cards, medical bills, or personal loans—and combining them into a single new loan or balance. Instead of five payments at five rates, you make one payment at one rate. The goal is usually one of three things: a lower interest rate, a single manageable payment, or a fixed payoff date.

It is not magic. You still owe the same underlying principal. Consolidation changes the terms and the number of bills, not the fact that the money has to be repaid.

## Common ways to consolidate

- **Personal loan:** borrow a lump sum at a fixed rate, use it to pay off the cards, then repay the loan in installments.
- **Balance transfer card:** move card balances to a new card, often with a 0% promo period, then pay it down before the rate jumps.
- **Home equity loan or HELOC:** borrow against home equity at a lower rate, using the house as collateral.
- **Debt management plan:** through a credit counseling agency that negotiates terms with creditors on your behalf.

Each has tradeoffs. Unsecured options do not risk your home but may have higher rates; secured options are cheaper but put an asset on the line. The [Debt Consolidation Calculator](/calculators/debt-consolidation-calculator/) compares your current total cost against a consolidation loan.

## A worked example

Suppose you have three cards totaling $12,000 at an average 23% APR, and you pay $400 a month. You might take years to clear it and pay a lot in interest. A consolidation loan at 12% for 36 months could cut the rate roughly in half and give you a firm payoff date.

The math only works if the new rate is clearly below your blended current rate and you actually pay the loan off. If the consolidation loan is 23% too, you have simplified your life without saving a dollar.

## When consolidation helps—and when it hurts

It helps when:

- the new rate is lower than your current blended rate
- you have a stable income to make the single payment
- you stop using the paid-off cards, so balances do not creep back

It hurts—or simply fails—when:

- you transfer balances but keep spending, ending up with old cards refilled plus the new loan
- the loan has fees that erase the rate savings
- you stretch the term so long that the lower payment costs more in total interest
- you put unsecured debt on your home and then risk foreclosure over credit-card spending

If motivation is your main struggle, the avalanche and snowball methods in [paying off credit card debt](/guides/paying-off-credit-card-debt) may serve you better than a new loan.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing the monthly payment, not the total cost.** A lower payment from a longer term can cost more overall.
- **Ignoring fees.** Balance-transfer fees, origination fees, and closing costs change the real savings.
- **Refilling the cards.** The classic failure: consolidate, then charge again. Free up the cards only after behavior changes.
- **Missing the promo deadline.** A 0% transfer card becomes expensive if a balance remains when the rate resets.

## How to consolidate well

1. **List every balance, rate, and minimum** so you know your true blended cost.
2. **Shop the consolidation offer** and compare total interest, fees, and term with the [Debt Consolidation Calculator](/calculators/debt-consolidation-calculator/).
3. **Close or freeze the old cards** if the temptation to recharge is real.
4. **Set the payoff date** and automate the new payment so you do not drift.
5. **Keep an emergency fund** so a surprise bill does not send you back to the cards.
