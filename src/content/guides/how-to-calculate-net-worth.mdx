## Key takeaway

Your net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe. It is the single clearest snapshot of where you stand financially, and tracking it over time matters more than the number on any one day.

## Net worth is the scoreboard, not the game

Add up what you own—cash, savings, retirement accounts, investments, the equity in your home, the resale value of your car—and subtract what you owe: mortgage, student loans, credit cards, auto loans, any other debt. The result is your net worth.

A simple formula:

> **Net worth = total assets − total liabilities**

If the number is positive, your assets exceed your debts. If it is negative, you owe more than you own. Both are normal at different life stages. A recent graduate with student loans often starts negative. That is a starting line, not a verdict.

## What counts as an asset

Be honest and a little conservative. Include:

- **Cash and bank accounts:** checking, savings, high-yield accounts.
- **Investments:** brokerage accounts, retirement accounts (401k, IRA, Roth), and the current value of any funds you hold.
- **Home equity:** your home's estimated value minus what you still owe on the mortgage. Use a realistic sale price, not the price you paid.
- **Other property:** a car, valuable jewelry, or collectibles—but value them at what you could actually sell them for, not what you paid.

Leave out things you could not quickly sell or that lose value fast, or at least mark them down. Your used couch is not really an asset.

## What counts as a liability

List every balance you are on the hook for:

- **Mortgage** and any home equity loans.
- **Student loans**, auto loans, and personal loans.
- **Credit card balances** and any "buy now, pay later" plans you have not paid off.
- **Medical or tax bills** you owe.

The goal is a true picture, so include the small balances too. A few forgotten subscriptions on credit do not move the number much, but a forgotten personal loan does.

## A worked example

Suppose a household has:

- $12,000 in savings and checking
- $60,000 in retirement accounts
- A home worth $400,000 with a $300,000 mortgage (so $100,000 equity)
- A car worth $18,000

Assets total $190,000. Liabilities are the $300,000 mortgage plus $8,000 in student loans and $4,000 on credit cards: $312,000.

Net worth = $190,000 − $312,000 = **−$122,000**.

That looks discouraging until you remember the home equity is already netted in: the mortgage is the liability, the $100,000 equity is the asset side. The negative comes from the other debts. Pay off the $12,000 of non-mortgage debt and the number swings by the same $12,000. Small, focused paydowns move the scoreboard fast.

## The mistake everyone makes: only checking once

A net worth number on one day tells you almost nothing. The trend over years is the real story. Someone with a "low" net worth who adds $400 a month and watches debt fall is winning. Someone with a big number who stopped saving is drifting.

Other common errors:

- **Counting the home at purchase price:** values move, so re-estimate yearly.
- **Forgetting retirement accounts:** they are usually your largest asset outside the home.
- **Ignoring the mortgage balance:** home equity is value minus what you owe, not the full price.
- **Letting one bad month scare you:** markets dip; the line wiggles. Watch the slope, not the wiggle.

## How to build it

1. **Calculate it once** using today's real balances—see the [Net Worth Calculator](/calculators/net-worth-calculator/).
2. **Automate savings and debt paydown** so the assets side grows and the liabilities side shrinks without weekly decisions.
3. **Re-check every quarter** and write the number down. The habit of measuring is what changes behavior.
4. **Celebrate the slope:** a rising line, even a slow one, is the signal you are on track.
