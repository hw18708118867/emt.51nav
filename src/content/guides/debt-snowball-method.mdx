## The debt snowball focuses on small wins first

The debt snowball is a debt payoff method where you pay off your debts from smallest to largest, regardless of interest rate. You make minimum payments on all debts, then put any extra money toward the smallest debt first. When that's paid off, you roll the money you were putting toward it into the next smallest debt, and so on.

The "snowball" refers to how your payment power grows as each debt is eliminated—what starts as a small extra payment can become a large one by the end, building momentum along the way.

## How the debt snowball works step by step

To use the debt snowball method:

1. **List all your debts** (except your mortgage, usually) from smallest balance to largest, ignoring interest rates.
2. **Make the minimum payment** on every debt every month.
3. **Put any extra money you can find** toward the smallest debt on the list.
4. **When the smallest debt is paid off**, take the money you were putting toward it and add it to the minimum payment for the next smallest debt.
5. **Repeat the process** until all debts are paid off.

The key is that each paid-off debt gives you a psychological win, which can help you stay motivated when the payoff process feels slow.

## Why the order matters for motivation

The debt snowball is built on behavioral finance, not just math. Research shows that people are more likely to stick with a debt payoff plan when they see progress quickly.

By paying off small debts first, you get frequent wins that keep you going. Even if you could save a little more interest with a different method, the snowball can be the better choice if it means you actually follow through and become debt-free.

## An example of the debt snowball in action

Suppose you have these debts:
- Credit Card A: $800 balance, 22% APR, $25 minimum payment
- Credit Card B: $2,500 balance, 18% APR, $60 minimum payment
- Personal Loan: $7,000 balance, 10% APR, $150 minimum payment
- Car Loan: $12,000 balance, 6% APR, $280 minimum payment

Total minimum payments: $515 per month. If you can put an extra $200 toward debt each month ($715 total), you'd:

1. Pay off Credit Card A first, in about 4 months
2. Then put that $25 + $200 extra toward Credit Card B, paying it off in about 8 more months
3. Then put that $60 + $225 extra toward the personal loan, paying it off in about 15 more months
4. Finally, put that $150 + $285 extra toward the car loan

Each step gives you a clear win and increases your payment power for the next debt.

## When the debt snowball shines

The debt snowball is particularly effective if:

- You have many small debts that feel overwhelming
- You've struggled to stay motivated with debt payoff in the past
- You value seeing quick progress more than saving every possible dollar in interest
- You have enough cash flow to make the minimums plus some extra each month

The method works because it turns a big, intimidating goal ("pay off $25,000 of debt") into a series of smaller, achievable goals ("pay off this $800 credit card first").

## What the debt snowball gives up in interest savings

Because the snowball focuses on balance rather than interest rate, it usually costs more in total interest than the debt avalanche method (which targets highest-interest debt first).

Whether that tradeoff is worth it depends on your personality and past behavior. If the avalanche's math makes sense but you never actually stick with it, the snowball's slightly higher cost might be a worthwhile investment in actually becoming debt-free.

## How to find extra money for the snowball

To make the snowball work, you need some extra money to put toward the smallest debt each month. Common places to find that extra money include:

- Reviewing your budget and cutting non-essential spending for a period
- Using a side gig or overtime income specifically for debt payoff
- Redirecting money that was going to other goals temporarily (while keeping a small emergency fund)
- Selling things you no longer need and putting the proceeds toward debt

Even an extra $50 or $100 per month can make a meaningful difference in how quickly you work through the list.

## Why you still need a small emergency fund

One common mistake when using the debt snowball is putting every spare dollar toward debt without keeping any cash buffer. This creates risk: if an unexpected expense comes up, you might have to put it on a credit card, undoing your progress.

Most people do better with a small starter emergency fund ($500-$1,000) while working through the debt snowball. It's not enough to cover everything, but it's enough to handle small surprises without derailing the plan.

## The debt snowball is about behavior, not perfection

The debt snowball isn't mathematically optimal in every situation, but it's often behaviorally optimal. It recognizes that paying off debt is as much about motivation and consistency as it is about interest rates.

The best debt payoff method isn't the one that saves the most interest on paper—it's the one you can actually stick with until you're debt-free.
