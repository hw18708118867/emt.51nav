## Key takeaway

You can claim Social Security as early as 62, at your full retirement age, or as late as 70. Claiming later pays a permanently larger monthly check; claiming earlier locks in a smaller one for life. The "best" age depends on your health, longevity, and need for the income.

## Social Security is a monthly promise with a timer

After a career of paying in, you earn the right to a monthly Social Security benefit. The amount is based on your highest 35 years of earnings. But when you start taking it changes the size of every check you ever receive.

- **Age 62 (earliest):** benefits are reduced, permanently, by about 30% compared with your full retirement age.
- **Full retirement age (FRA):** roughly 66 to 67 depending on your birth year—this is your "unreduced" benefit.
- **Age 70 (latest):** benefits are increased, by about 8% per year past FRA, up to a permanently larger check—roughly 24% to 32% more than at FRA.

There is no increase after 70, so waiting beyond 70 gains you nothing.

## The break-even question

The core trade-off: claim early and you get more checks but smaller ones; claim late and you get fewer checks but bigger ones. At some age, the two paths pay out the same total. That crossing point is your break-even, usually somewhere in your early 80s.

A simplified example: suppose your FRA benefit is $2,000 a month.

- Claim at 62: about $1,400/month. By age 82 you have collected roughly $336,000 (240 months × $1,400).
- Claim at 70: about $2,480/month. By age 82 you have collected roughly $297,600 (120 months × $2,480).

Around 82–83 the later claimer pulls ahead. Live longer and delaying wins; die earlier and claiming early wins. That is the bet, and it is why health and family history matter.

## The mistake everyone makes: claiming on emotion

People claim at 62 because they "want it before it changes" or because a parent died young. Those are real fears, but the math often favors waiting if you have other income and reasonable health.

Other errors:

- **Forgetting the spouse:** a higher-earning spouse's delayed claim can leave the survivor a larger benefit for life.
- **Ignoring taxes:** benefits can be taxable above certain income levels.
- **Claiming while still working early:** earnings above a limit can temporarily reduce your benefit before FRA.
- **Assuming 70 is always best:** if you need the money or have a short life expectancy, earlier can be rational.

## How to decide

1. **Estimate your benefit at 62, FRA, and 70** with the [Social Security Estimator](/calculators/social-security-estimator/).
2. **Find your break-even** with the [Social Security Break-Even Calculator](/calculators/social-security-breakeven-calculator/).
3. **Pressure-test your health and family history**—longevity runs in the family argues for waiting.
4. **Check other income:** if you have a pension or savings covering early retirement, delaying Social Security is cheaper insurance.
5. **Coordinate with your spouse** so the higher earner's claim supports the survivor.
