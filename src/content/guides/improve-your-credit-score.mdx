## Key takeaway

Most credit-score gains come from boring, repeatable habits: pay on time, lower what you owe, and let old accounts age. There is no shortcut, but the right actions move the number faster than people expect.

## Start with the report, not the score

Before you try to improve anything, pull your credit reports and confirm they are accurate. The score is just a summary of what is in those files, so if the report is wrong, the score is wrong too. Common errors include accounts that are not yours, balances reported higher than reality, and old negative items that should have aged off.

You can get the reports and review them using [how to check your credit reports for free](/blog/check-credit-reports-for-free). Dispute anything inaccurate with the bureau reporting it—fixing a real error can lift a score quickly, sometimes within a billing cycle.

## Pay on time, every time

Payment history is the largest slice of the score, so on-time payments are the foundation. If you have missed payments, the damage fades with time as long as you stay current. A few practical moves:

- **Autopay the minimum** on every account so a busy week never becomes a late mark.
- **Stack due dates** near a predictable payday so you are never caught short.
- **Catch up fast** if you slip. A payment that is 30 days late hurts; one that is 60 or 90 days late hurts more. Bringing an account current stops the bleeding.

If cash flow is the issue, the [Budget Calculator](/calculators/budget-calculator/) helps find the money to cover minimums before they are late.

## Lower your balances

The second-biggest factor is how much you owe versus your limits. Lowering utilization is one of the fastest levers because utilization updates as issuers report new balances.

- **Pay before the statement date** so a lower balance is what gets reported.
- **Ask for a higher limit** on an existing card you use responsibly; a bigger limit lowers utilization without spending more—just do not then charge more.
- **Spread spending** across cards so no single one shows high utilization.
- **Target the highest-utilization card first** even if it is not the highest rate, for the biggest reported drop.

## Let time do its work

Two factors reward patience: length of history and the aging of old negative marks. You cannot rush these, but you can avoid hurting them:

- **Keep old accounts open.** Closing a long-held card shortens your history and can raise utilization.
- **Don't chase new accounts.** Each application can add a hard inquiry, and a burst of applications looks risky. Space applications out by many months.
- **Outlast old mistakes.** Most negative items matter less with each passing year, and the score slowly recovers as long as recent behavior is clean.

## Common mistakes that stall progress

- **Closing cards to "be responsible."** It often backfires by raising utilization and shortening history.
- **Paying only the minimum on high balances.** You stay current (good) but utilization stays high (less good). Pay more when you can.
- **Falling for credit-repair ads** that promise to delete accurate information. You can dispute errors for free; no service can remove true, timely negative items.
- **Ignoring the score's blind spots.** Income and savings do not build credit, so a strong saver with thin credit can still have a weak score.

## A simple 90-day plan

1. **Week 1:** pull all three reports and dispute errors.
2. **Month 1:** set autopay on every account; pay down the highest-utilization card.
3. **Month 2:** request a limit increase on a card you use well; pay before statement dates.
4. **Month 3:** check the reports again to confirm balances updated and disputes resolved.
