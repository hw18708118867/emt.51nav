## Why refinancing is a math question, not a rate question

When rates fall, headlines shout "refinance now." When rates rise, everyone says wait. Neither is right for you until you run the break-even. Refinancing only pays off if the savings outlast the cost before you sell or refinance again.

This guide covers when refinancing makes sense, how to compute the break-even point, and which costs to include so the decision is honest.

## Step 1: Know what refinancing replaces

A refinance pays off your old mortgage with a new loan, ideally at a lower rate, a shorter term, or both. The goal is usually one of:

- lower the monthly payment by reducing the rate
- shorten the term (for example 30 to 15 years) to save total interest
- switch from an adjustable to a fixed rate for stability
- tap equity with a cash-out refinance (use with caution)

The [Refinance Calculator](/calculators/refinance-calculator/) compares your current loan to a proposed new one.

## Step 2: Calculate the break-even point first

The break-even point is the number of months it takes for monthly savings to repay the refinance closing costs. If costs are $4,000 and you save $150 a month, the break-even is about 27 months.

- break-even under ~24 months is strong
- 24 to 48 months is reasonable if you will stay
- beyond 48 months, only refinance if you are confident you will stay long term

If you sell or refinance again before break-even, you lose money on the deal.

## Step 3: Include the full cost, not just the rate

Closing costs on a refinance commonly run 2% to 5% of the new loan. They can include:

- lender origination and underwriting fees
- appraisal and title fees
- discount points if you buy down the rate
- prepaid interest and escrow funding

A lower rate with high costs can have a worse break-even than a slightly higher rate with low costs. The [Refinance Calculator](/calculators/refinance-calculator/) lets you enter both to see the real net savings.

## Step 4: Watch the loan term reset trap

A common mistake is refinancing a 30-year loan you are five years into, back into a new 30-year loan. Your payment may drop, but you restart the clock and can pay more total interest over time.

If your aim is saving interest, refinance into a term that keeps or shortens your remaining payoff window. The [Extra Mortgage Payments Calculator](/calculators/extra-mortgage-payments-calculator/) shows how additional payments change the total cost.

## Step 5: Consider a shorter term instead of just a lower rate

Refinancing from 30 to 15 years usually carries a lower rate and saves large interest, but raises the monthly payment. The right move depends on cash flow:

- if the higher payment still leaves a comfortable margin, a shorter term is efficient
- if it strains your budget, keep the longer term and pay extra when you can

## Step 6: Be cautious with cash-out refinancing

A cash-out refinance borrows more than you owe and gives you the difference. It can make sense for a low-rate need like a renovation, but it:

- increases your loan balance and total interest
- resets or extends your term
- turns consumer spending into secured debt against your home

Compare it against other options before tapping home equity. The [How Much House Can You Afford calculator](/calculators/how-much-house-can-you-afford/) helps keep the new payment realistic.

## A realistic example

A homeowner with a $300,000 balance at 7% on 28 years remaining refinances to 6% on a new 30-year loan. The payment drops, but total interest over the longer term may rise. If instead they refinance to a 20-year term at 6%, the payment may stay similar while total interest falls. The calculator shows which path actually saves.

## Common refinancing mistakes

- Refinancing without computing the break-even point.
- Resetting to a full 30-year term and paying more interest overall.
- Ignoring closing costs in the savings math.
- Chasing a tiny rate drop that never recovers its cost.
- Using cash-out refinance for discretionary spending.
