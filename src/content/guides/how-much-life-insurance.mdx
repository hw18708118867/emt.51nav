## The life insurance question people actually have

Most life insurance content answers "what is term life" when buyers really want "how much do I need, and is it worth it." The amount is the harder, more personal question, and the answer is about replacing income and covering obligations, not hitting a magic multiple.

This guide shows how to size a policy, choose a type, and avoid the common oversell, with the calculator that turns the estimate into a number.

## Step 1: Start with who depends on your income

If no one relies on your income, you may need little or no life insurance. The need appears when someone would face a financial hole if you were gone:

- a spouse who could not cover the mortgage alone
- young children who need support for years
- a co-signed debt or business obligation
- aging parents you support

The bigger the gap your absence would create, the more coverage matters.

## Step 2: Estimate the income replacement

A common starting point is 10 to 15 times your annual income while dependents are young. But the multiple is a shortcut, not the answer. A cleaner method:

- add up the income your family would lose each year
- multiply by the number of years of support needed (for example until kids are independent)
- add large one-time obligations: mortgage balance, other debts, future education costs
- subtract existing savings and current coverage

## Step 3: Choose term for most families

For the vast majority of households, **term life** is the right tool:

- it covers a fixed period (10, 20, or 30 years) when the need is highest
- premiums are far lower than permanent insurance for the same death benefit
- the savings can be invested elsewhere for better total return

**Whole or permanent life** combines insurance with a savings component, costs much more, and is usually only worth it for specific estate or permanent-need situations. ## Step 4: Match the term to the need

Pick a term that covers the years of highest dependence:

- young kids at home: 20 or 30 years
- a mortgage you want covered: match the remaining term
- empty-nesters with grown, independent children: often little or no new coverage needed

As savings and the mortgage shrink, the insurance need falls on its own, which is why a level term that expires is usually fine.

## Step 5: Cover both earners, including at home

A non-working parent often has no salary but huge economic value: childcare, transportation, household management. Replacing that with paid help is a real cost, so stay-at-home parents are often underinsured. Estimate the annual cost of those services and include it in the need.

## Step 6: Avoid the common oversell

Sales pressure pushes permanent policies because they pay bigger commissions. Watch for:

- being sold whole life when term fits
- being told you must invest through insurance to be "protected"
- policies sized to a multiple with no link to your actual obligations
- ignoring the cheaper term option that covers the same need

Buy the need, not the product.

## A realistic example

A couple, both 32, with a $280,000 mortgage and two kids aged 3 and 5:

- each earns $70,000
- they want income replaced for about 15 years and the mortgage covered
- a rough need per earner is around 12 times income plus the mortgage share
- two 20-year term policies at that size cost far less than one permanent policy

The coverage steps down naturally as the kids become independent and the mortgage shrinks.

## Common life insurance mistakes

- Buying whole life when term covers the need for a fraction of the cost.
- Sizing to a multiple with no link to real obligations.
- Insuring the earner but not the at-home parent.
- Letting a policy lapse after the need has passed, or keeping unneeded permanent coverage.
- Confusing investing with insuring.
