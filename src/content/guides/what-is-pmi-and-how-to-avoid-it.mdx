## PMI protects the lender, not you

Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI) covers the lender if you stop paying your mortgage. It doesn't protect you or your equity.

What it *does* do is let you buy a home with less than 20% down. In exchange for that flexibility, you pay an extra monthly cost until you have enough equity that the lender no longer sees you as extra risk.

## How PMI is calculated

PMI costs typically range from 0.5% to 1.5% of the original loan amount per year, split into monthly payments. The exact rate depends on:

- your credit score
- your down payment percentage
- the loan type
- whether you pay PMI monthly or upfront

For example, on a $400,000 loan with a 0.75% PMI rate, you'd pay $3,000 per year, or $250 per month, until you hit the threshold to remove it.

## The 20% equity threshold, roughly

The most common way to remove PMI is when you reach 20% equity in your home, either through paying down the principal or home value appreciation, or both.

With 20% down, you can usually avoid PMI entirely from the start. If you put less down, you can request PMI removal once your loan balance hits 80% of the original home value. For many loans, PMI cancels automatically at 78% loan-to-value as long as you're current on payments.

These thresholds refer to the original appraised value unless you get a new appraisal to show appreciation.

## Ways to avoid PMI without 20% down

If you can't put 20% down but want to avoid monthly PMI, there are alternatives:

- **Lender-paid mortgage insurance (LPMI):** The lender pays the PMI upfront in exchange for a slightly higher interest rate. This can make sense if you plan to stay in the home for a shorter period.
- **Piggyback second mortgage:** You take out a first mortgage for 80% of the home value, then a second mortgage (often a HELOC) for the portion between 80% and your down payment. This avoids PMI but means two loan payments.
- **Single-premium PMI:** You pay the entire PMI cost upfront at closing, either in cash or by rolling it into the loan. This works if you have extra cash at closing but not enough for a full 20% down payment.
- **Doctor, lawyer, and professional loans:** Some professions qualify for special loan programs that let low down payments avoid PMI.

Each option trades one cost structure for another, so it's worth comparing the total cost over the time you expect to keep the loan.

## How extra payments affect PMI timing

Every extra payment you make toward principal gets you to the PMI removal threshold faster. Because the threshold is based on the loan balance relative to the original home value, extra principal payments directly accelerate the timeline.

This is one area where extra mortgage payments deliver double benefit: they reduce your total interest cost *and* they eliminate an ongoing monthly expense sooner.

## When appreciation helps remove PMI faster

If your home value increases significantly, you might be able to remove PMI earlier by getting a new appraisal showing that your current loan balance is now less than 80% of the *current* value rather than the original purchase price.

Not all lenders allow this, and you usually need to have the loan for at least two years before using appreciation to remove PMI. It's worth checking your loan terms before paying for an appraisal.

## PMI isn't forever, but it matters while you have it

PMI can feel frustrating because it's a monthly cost that doesn't build your equity. But it serves a real purpose: it lets people become homeowners earlier than they otherwise could.

The key is to treat PMI as a temporary cost rather than a permanent one. Track your loan balance, know when you hit the removal threshold, and consider extra principal payments if getting there faster would save you meaningful money each month.
