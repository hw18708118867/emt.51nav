## Key takeaway

Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) are the amounts the IRS makes you withdraw from tax-deferred retirement accounts each year after you turn 73. Miss one and the penalty is steep—so know your number before the deadline.

## RMDs are the IRS taking its turn

You got a tax break putting money into a traditional 401(k) or IRA: no tax on the contribution, and the money grew untaxed. The deal has a deadline. Starting at age 73 (for most people), the IRS requires you to take a minimum withdrawal each year and pay ordinary income tax on it. That required amount is the RMD.

The rule applies to tax-deferred accounts: traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s, and similar. It does **not** apply to Roth IRAs during your lifetime—Roth accounts have no RMDs for the original owner, which is one reason they are valuable for leaving money to heirs.

## How the amount is calculated

Each year, the RMD is roughly your account balance on December 31 of the prior year divided by a life-expectancy factor from the IRS tables.

> **RMD = prior year-end balance ÷ distribution period**

The distribution period comes from IRS life-expectancy tables and gets smaller as you age, so the percentage you must take grows over time. At 73 the divisor is about 26.5, meaning you withdraw roughly 3.8% of the balance. By 80 it is closer to 5.4%, by 90 around 8.9%.

A quick example: a $500,000 IRA balance at the end of the year before you turn 73 gives an RMD near $500,000 ÷ 26.5 ≈ **$18,870** for that first year. That amount is taxable income.

## The penalty is the part that hurts

If you fail to take the full RMD, the amount you should have taken but did not is subject to a penalty. The penalty was historically 50% of the shortfall—still a powerful incentive to get it right, even after recent reforms lowered it. The safe move is to automate the withdrawal so it never slips.

Two practical notes:

- **Your first RMD can wait until April 1 of the year after you turn 73**, but if you delay, you then owe two in that following year, which can push you into a higher tax bracket.
- **Still working?** You may be able to delay RMDs from your current employer's 401(k), but not from an IRA. Rules differ, so check the plan.

## The mistake everyone makes: forgetting the tax hit

RMDs are ordinary income. A large withdrawal can bump you into a higher bracket, increase Medicare premiums (which are income-tested), and affect the taxability of Social Security. People are surprised in year one.

Other errors:

- **Assuming Roth IRAs have RMDs:** they do not, for the original owner.
- **Taking it from the wrong account:** you can aggregate IRAs and take the total from one, but 401(k)s must be calculated and taken separately per plan.
- **Reinvesting without a plan:** the cash leaves the account taxed; where it goes next should be intentional.
- **Missing the deadline:** there is no grace period that saves the penalty automatically.

## How to handle RMDs well

1. **Know your number before the year starts** with the [RMD Calculator](/calculators/rmd-calculator/).
2. **Automate the withdrawal** on a schedule so the deadline never sneaks up.
3. **Plan for the tax**—set aside or withhold, don't spend the gross amount.
4. **Consider qualified charitable distributions (QCDs)** if you are charitably inclined; they can satisfy the RMD and reduce taxable income.
