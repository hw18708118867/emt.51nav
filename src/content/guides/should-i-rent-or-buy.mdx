## The question most people ask the wrong way

"Should I rent or buy?" usually gets answered with a slogan: buying builds wealth, renting is throwing money away. Both are wrong as blanket rules. The honest answer depends on your numbers, your time horizon, and how stable your life is.

This guide walks through the real rent-versus-buy decision using the costs on each side, then points you to the calculator that turns it into a number you can act on.

## Step 1: Put the two options on equal footing

Compare the same home, not a fancy purchase against a cheap rental. The fair test is: what does it cost to live in this home if you rent it versus if you own it, over the same years?

On the rent side you pay rent and renters insurance, and you can invest the cash you did not spend on a down payment. On the own side you pay the mortgage (principal and interest), property taxes, insurance, PMI if under 20% down, maintenance, and closing costs, but you build equity and may get tax benefits.

The [Rent vs Buy Calculator](/calculators/rent-vs-buy-calculator/) lines these up side by side so the comparison is apples to apples.

## Step 2: Count the costs renters forget they avoid

Owners carry costs renters do not:

- maintenance and repairs (often 1% to 3% of home value per year)
- property tax increases
- HOA dues
- the interest portion of early mortgage payments, which is mostly cost, not equity
- the opportunity cost of the down payment sitting in the house instead of invested

Renters trade those for one predictable number and flexibility. That is not "throwing money away"; it is buying flexibility and avoiding risk.

## Step 3: Count the wealth owners build

Ownership builds wealth through:

- principal paydown with every payment
- price appreciation if the local market rises
- tax deductions on mortgage interest and property taxes (where itemizing beats the standard deduction)
- forced savings, since you cannot easily spend your home equity

But equity is illiquid. You cannot tap it without selling or borrowing against it, and selling carries transaction costs of roughly 6% to 10%.

## Step 4: Use the break-even horizon

The single most useful number is the break-even point: how many years you must stay before owning beats renting. Buying almost always loses if you move within two or three years because transaction costs eat the equity you built.

As a rough rule:

- staying 1 to 3 years usually favors renting
- staying 5 to 7 years is the typical break-even zone
- staying 10-plus years usually favors buying, all else equal

## Step 5: Factor in your life, not just the math

Two people with identical numbers can have opposite answers:

- if your job or city may change soon, renting protects you
- if you have kids in a school district you love, stability has value beyond dollars
- if you dislike handling repairs, the mental load of ownership is real
- if you want to control and customize your space, ownership delivers that

The numbers set the frame; your life sets the answer.

## Step 6: Stress-test the price and rent assumptions

Small assumption changes swing the result:

- a higher rent growth rate makes buying look better
- a flat or falling local market makes buying worse
- a lower down payment raises PMI and interest, favoring renting
- a long stay compounds ownership's advantage

Run a few scenarios in the [Rent vs Buy Calculator](/calculators/rent-vs-buy-calculator/) rather than trusting one set of inputs.

## A realistic example

A $400,000 home with 10% down at a 6.5% rate, in a market where a comparable rental is $2,400 a month:

- owning all-in might run about $3,100 a month plus maintenance
- renting leaves roughly $700 a month that could be invested
- the break-even is often around 5 to 6 years

If the buyer stays 10 years and the market rises modestly, ownership wins. If they move in year 3, renting almost certainly wins.

## Common rent-vs-buy mistakes

- Comparing a dream purchase to a bare-bones rental.
- Ignoring transaction costs when you eventually sell.
- Treating the mortgage payment as all equity (interest is the cost).
- Assuming appreciation is guaranteed.
- Letting slogans decide a personal math question.
